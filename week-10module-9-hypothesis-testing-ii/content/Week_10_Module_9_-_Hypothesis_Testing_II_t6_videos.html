Introduction

Running a hypothesis test is only half the job. The other half — and the part that engineers are judged on in reports, design reviews, and publications — is explaining what the result actually means. This topic focuses on the chain of reasoning that connects a test statistic to a p-value, a p-value to a decision at a chosen significance level, and that decision to a clear, honest statement about the engineering question you started with.

Along the way you will look closely at what a p-value is and is not. It is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours assuming the null hypothesis is true; it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, not the probability that you made a mistake, and not a measure of how large or important an effect is. Confidence intervals help here: they carry the same information as the test but express it on the scale of the quantity you care about, which makes it much easier to separate statistical significance from practical significance.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how the test statistic is computed and what it measures in standard-error units, exactly how the p-value is read off a z-table or software output and compared to alpha, and the precise wording used for conclusions ("reject" versus "fail to reject," and never "prove"). Notice also any cautions the presenters give about common misreadings, since those are the errors that most often show up in written reports.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

These videos reinforce the interpretation side of the module, especially MO4. The first provides the computational bridge from a test statistic to a p-value, supporting MO2 by making clear what the statistic measures and how its magnitude drives the resulting probability. The second places that calculation inside a full testing procedure, which helps when you must justify your choice of test and assumptions under MO1 and MO3 and then write up the result.
