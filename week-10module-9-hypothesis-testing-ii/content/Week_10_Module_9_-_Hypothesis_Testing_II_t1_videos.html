Introduction

In Week 9 you tested claims about a single population parameter. Engineering questions, though, are usually comparative: Does the new alloy last longer than the old one? Does supplier B's bearing diameter differ from supplier A's? Two-sample hypothesis tests answer these questions by asking whether an observed difference between two sample means is large enough to be inconsistent with the hypothesis that the two populations have the same mean.

The logic is the same as before, but the sampling distribution now describes a difference of means. The null hypothesis is typically that the difference is zero, the standard error combines variability from both groups, and the test statistic measures how many standard errors the observed difference sits from the hypothesized value. Which statistic you use depends on what you know: if both population standard deviations are known (or samples are large), a two-sample z-test applies; if the standard deviations are estimated from the data, a two-sample t-test with an appropriate degrees-of-freedom rule applies. Underlying both is the assumption that the two samples are independent of each other and drawn randomly, with approximate normality or large enough samples for the Central Limit Theorem to help.

As you watch the videos below, track four things in every worked example: how the hypotheses are written in terms of the difference of means, how the standard error is built from the two sample sizes, where the degrees of freedom come from in the t-test, and how the final p-value or critical-value comparison is translated into a conclusion stated in the language of the original problem. Pay particular attention to the moment each presenter decides whether the samples are independent, because that single judgment determines whether this module's methods apply at all.
