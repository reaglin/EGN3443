Two-Sample Hypothesis Tests

Introduction

In Week 9 you tested claims about a single population parameter. Engineering questions, though, are usually comparative: Does the new alloy last longer than the old one? Does supplier B's bearing diameter differ from supplier A's? Two-sample hypothesis tests answer these questions by asking whether an observed difference between two sample means is large enough to be inconsistent with the hypothesis that the two populations have the same mean.

The logic is the same as before, but the sampling distribution now describes a difference of means. The null hypothesis is typically that the difference is zero, the standard error combines variability from both groups, and the test statistic measures how many standard errors the observed difference sits from the hypothesized value. Which statistic you use depends on what you know: if both population standard deviations are known (or samples are large), a two-sample z-test applies; if the standard deviations are estimated from the data, a two-sample t-test with an appropriate degrees-of-freedom rule applies. Underlying both is the assumption that the two samples are independent of each other and drawn randomly, with approximate normality or large enough samples for the Central Limit Theorem to help.

As you watch the videos below, track four things in every worked example: how the hypotheses are written in terms of the difference of means, how the standard error is built from the two sample sizes, where the degrees of freedom come from in the t-test, and how the final p-value or critical-value comparison is translated into a conclusion stated in the language of the original problem. Pay particular attention to the moment each presenter decides whether the samples are independent, because that single judgment determines whether this module's methods apply at all.

Paired Sample Comparisons

Introduction

Many engineering experiments do not produce two unrelated groups of measurements. Instead, the same specimen, machine, operator, or test site is measured twice — before and after a treatment, with two different instruments, or under two operating conditions. Designs like these are called paired or matched designs, and they require a different analysis than the independent two-sample tests you studied earlier in this module.

The key idea is simple but powerful: when observations come in natural pairs, you analyze the differences within each pair rather than the two sets of raw measurements. Each pair contributes one difference score d, and the analysis reduces to a familiar one-sample t-test on those differences, with n - 1 degrees of freedom where n is the number of pairs. Because each unit serves as its own control, variability that comes from differences between units (a stiffer beam, a hotter furnace, a more experienced operator) cancels out, so the test can detect smaller real effects with fewer observations.

As you watch the videos, focus on four things: how the author recognizes that data are paired rather than independent, how the difference column is computed and in which direction, which formula and degrees of freedom are used for the test statistic, and how the conclusion is stated in terms of the mean difference. Also note the assumptions being relied on — pairs must be independent of each other and the differences should be approximately normal (or the sample of pairs large enough) — and watch for how a confidence interval on the mean difference complements the p-value when you report results.

Selecting the Appropriate Statistical Test

Introduction

By this point in the course you have seen several hypothesis tests: the two-sample z-test, the two-sample t-test (pooled and unpooled), and the paired-sample t-test. The harder skill is not computing a test statistic but deciding which test belongs with which problem. This topic gives you a structured decision-making framework: start with the type of data you collected (continuous measurement, count, or category), then ask about sample size and distributional assumptions, whether your samples are independent or paired, how many groups you are comparing, and finally what the research or engineering question actually asks.

This matters because an incorrectly chosen test produces a p-value that looks perfectly respectable but answers the wrong question. In engineering practice, where you may be comparing two production lines, two measurement instruments, or before-and-after readings on the same specimens, the difference between an independent two-sample test and a paired test can flip your conclusion. The framework also tells you when to step outside the parametric family entirely and reach for a non-parametric alternative such as the Mann-Whitney or Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running checklist. For each test mentioned, note: what data type it requires, how many groups it handles, whether it assumes independence or pairing, and which distributional assumptions must hold. Pay particular attention to the branch points in each decision tree, since those branch points are exactly the questions you should ask yourself on an exam problem or in the lab. The third video addresses a specific assumption check that feeds directly back into the two-sample t-test decision.

Assumptions and Conditions for Validity

Introduction

Every hypothesis test you have learned in this course rests on a set of assumptions. A t-statistic only follows a t-distribution, and a p-value only means what we claim it means, if certain conditions about the data hold: observations are independent, the underlying populations are approximately normal (or the samples are large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to help), and, for some versions of the two-sample test, the population variances are comparable. When those conditions fail, the arithmetic still produces a number, but the conclusion drawn from it can be badly wrong.

This topic asks you to slow down before computing and ask: is this test appropriate for this data? You will look at how independence is established by study design rather than by a test, how normality is checked informally with histograms, boxplots, and Q-Q plots or formally with tests such as Shapiro-Wilk, how equal variances are assessed (and why Welch's correction often makes that check less critical), and what changes when the data are paired — in that case the normality assumption applies to the differences, not to the two original samples. You will also see what to do when assumptions clearly fail, namely turn to non-parametric alternatives such as the Mann-Whitney U or Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The three videos below all walk through two-sample and paired t-tests with assumption checking built into the workflow. As you watch, pay attention to the order of operations: which assumptions are checked first, which diagnostic tool is used for each, what the presenter does when an assumption appears violated, and how the assumption check is eventually reported alongside the result. Two of the videos use R, so focus on the logic of each step rather than memorizing syntax.

Introduction to Non-Parametric Methods

Introduction

So far this module you have worked with tests that rest on fairly specific assumptions: that the underlying population is approximately normal, that variances behave in a particular way, and that the mean is a meaningful summary of the data. Real engineering data does not always cooperate. Sample sizes are small, measurements are skewed or contain outliers, and sometimes the data are ranks or ordered categories rather than true measurements. Non-parametric methods are the family of procedures designed for exactly these situations — they make far fewer assumptions about the shape of the population distribution, and many of them work with ranks or signs rather than raw values.

This topic is the bridge between knowing how to run a two-sample or paired t-test and knowing what to do when you check the assumptions and find they fail. You will learn what "non-parametric" actually means, the circumstances that call for these methods, what you gain (robustness to outliers and non-normality, applicability to ordinal data) and what you give up (typically some statistical power when the parametric assumptions would have held, and conclusions phrased about medians or distributions rather than means). You will also meet the standard pairings: the Wilcoxon rank-sum or Mann-Whitney U test as the counterpart to the two-sample t-test, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as the counterpart to the paired t-test, and the Kruskal-Wallis test in place of one-way ANOVA.

As you watch, keep a decision-making lens. Note the specific conditions each presenter cites as a reason to abandon the parametric test, write down the parametric/non-parametric pairs as they appear, and pay attention to how the null hypothesis is worded differently for a rank-based test. You are not being asked to memorize computational formulas here — the goal is to be able to justify, in a sentence or two, why a given engineering dataset calls for one approach over the other.

Interpreting and Communicating Results

Introduction

Running a hypothesis test is only half the job. The other half — and the part that engineers are judged on in reports, design reviews, and publications — is explaining what the result actually means. This topic focuses on the chain of reasoning that connects a test statistic to a p-value, a p-value to a decision at a chosen significance level, and that decision to a clear, honest statement about the engineering question you started with.

Along the way you will look closely at what a p-value is and is not. It is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours assuming the null hypothesis is true; it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, not the probability that you made a mistake, and not a measure of how large or important an effect is. Confidence intervals help here: they carry the same information as the test but express it on the scale of the quantity you care about, which makes it much easier to separate statistical significance from practical significance.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how the test statistic is computed and what it measures in standard-error units, exactly how the p-value is read off a z-table or software output and compared to alpha, and the precise wording used for conclusions ("reject" versus "fail to reject," and never "prove"). Notice also any cautions the presenters give about common misreadings, since those are the errors that most often show up in written reports.
