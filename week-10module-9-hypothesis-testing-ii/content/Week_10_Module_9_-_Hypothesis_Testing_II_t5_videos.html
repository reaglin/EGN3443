Introduction

So far this module you have worked with tests that rest on fairly specific assumptions: that the underlying population is approximately normal, that variances behave in a particular way, and that the mean is a meaningful summary of the data. Real engineering data does not always cooperate. Sample sizes are small, measurements are skewed or contain outliers, and sometimes the data are ranks or ordered categories rather than true measurements. Non-parametric methods are the family of procedures designed for exactly these situations — they make far fewer assumptions about the shape of the population distribution, and many of them work with ranks or signs rather than raw values.

This topic is the bridge between knowing how to run a two-sample or paired t-test and knowing what to do when you check the assumptions and find they fail. You will learn what "non-parametric" actually means, the circumstances that call for these methods, what you gain (robustness to outliers and non-normality, applicability to ordinal data) and what you give up (typically some statistical power when the parametric assumptions would have held, and conclusions phrased about medians or distributions rather than means). You will also meet the standard pairings: the Wilcoxon rank-sum or Mann-Whitney U test as the counterpart to the two-sample t-test, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as the counterpart to the paired t-test, and the Kruskal-Wallis test in place of one-way ANOVA.

As you watch, keep a decision-making lens. Note the specific conditions each presenter cites as a reason to abandon the parametric test, write down the parametric/non-parametric pairs as they appear, and pay attention to how the null hypothesis is worded differently for a rank-based test. You are not being asked to memorize computational formulas here — the goal is to be able to justify, in a sentence or two, why a given engineering dataset calls for one approach over the other.
