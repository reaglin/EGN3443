Introduction

By this point in the course you have seen several hypothesis tests: the two-sample z-test, the two-sample t-test (pooled and unpooled), and the paired-sample t-test. The harder skill is not computing a test statistic but deciding which test belongs with which problem. This topic gives you a structured decision-making framework: start with the type of data you collected (continuous measurement, count, or category), then ask about sample size and distributional assumptions, whether your samples are independent or paired, how many groups you are comparing, and finally what the research or engineering question actually asks.

This matters because an incorrectly chosen test produces a p-value that looks perfectly respectable but answers the wrong question. In engineering practice, where you may be comparing two production lines, two measurement instruments, or before-and-after readings on the same specimens, the difference between an independent two-sample test and a paired test can flip your conclusion. The framework also tells you when to step outside the parametric family entirely and reach for a non-parametric alternative such as the Mann-Whitney or Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running checklist. For each test mentioned, note: what data type it requires, how many groups it handles, whether it assumes independence or pairing, and which distributional assumptions must hold. Pay particular attention to the branch points in each decision tree, since those branch points are exactly the questions you should ask yourself on an exam problem or in the lab. The third video addresses a specific assumption check that feeds directly back into the two-sample t-test decision.
