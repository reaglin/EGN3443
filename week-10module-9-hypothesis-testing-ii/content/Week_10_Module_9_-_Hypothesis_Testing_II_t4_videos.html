Introduction

Every hypothesis test you have learned in this course rests on a set of assumptions. A t-statistic only follows a t-distribution, and a p-value only means what we claim it means, if certain conditions about the data hold: observations are independent, the underlying populations are approximately normal (or the samples are large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to help), and, for some versions of the two-sample test, the population variances are comparable. When those conditions fail, the arithmetic still produces a number, but the conclusion drawn from it can be badly wrong.

This topic asks you to slow down before computing and ask: is this test appropriate for this data? You will look at how independence is established by study design rather than by a test, how normality is checked informally with histograms, boxplots, and Q-Q plots or formally with tests such as Shapiro-Wilk, how equal variances are assessed (and why Welch's correction often makes that check less critical), and what changes when the data are paired — in that case the normality assumption applies to the differences, not to the two original samples. You will also see what to do when assumptions clearly fail, namely turn to non-parametric alternatives such as the Mann-Whitney U or Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The three videos below all walk through two-sample and paired t-tests with assumption checking built into the workflow. As you watch, pay attention to the order of operations: which assumptions are checked first, which diagnostic tool is used for each, what the presenter does when an assumption appears violated, and how the assumption check is eventually reported alongside the result. Two of the videos use R, so focus on the logic of each step rather than memorizing syntax.
