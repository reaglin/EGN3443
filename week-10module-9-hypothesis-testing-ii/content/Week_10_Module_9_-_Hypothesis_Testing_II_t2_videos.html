Introduction

Many engineering experiments do not produce two unrelated groups of measurements. Instead, the same specimen, machine, operator, or test site is measured twice — before and after a treatment, with two different instruments, or under two operating conditions. Designs like these are called paired or matched designs, and they require a different analysis than the independent two-sample tests you studied earlier in this module.

The key idea is simple but powerful: when observations come in natural pairs, you analyze the differences within each pair rather than the two sets of raw measurements. Each pair contributes one difference score d, and the analysis reduces to a familiar one-sample t-test on those differences, with n - 1 degrees of freedom where n is the number of pairs. Because each unit serves as its own control, variability that comes from differences between units (a stiffer beam, a hotter furnace, a more experienced operator) cancels out, so the test can detect smaller real effects with fewer observations.

As you watch the videos, focus on four things: how the author recognizes that data are paired rather than independent, how the difference column is computed and in which direction, which formula and degrees of freedom are used for the test statistic, and how the conclusion is stated in terms of the mean difference. Also note the assumptions being relied on — pairs must be independent of each other and the differences should be approximately normal (or the sample of pairs large enough) — and watch for how a confidence interval on the mean difference complements the p-value when you report results.
