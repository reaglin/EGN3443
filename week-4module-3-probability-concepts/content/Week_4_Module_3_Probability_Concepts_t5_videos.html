Introduction

A probability is a number, but a number by itself rarely helps anyone make a decision. This topic is about the step that comes after the arithmetic: saying clearly what a probability of 0.03, 0.5, or 0.97 actually means for the situation in front of you. That includes describing likelihood in plain language, explaining what the complement tells you, and being careful about whether a value came from theory (counting equally likely outcomes in a sample space) or from experiment (observing relative frequencies over many trials).

This matters in EGN3443 because engineers are usually not the final decision-maker acting alone. You will report reliability estimates, failure chances, and test results to managers, clients, regulators, and the public. A well-calculated probability that is poorly communicated — or that is quietly conditional on something the audience does not know about — can lead to a bad decision just as easily as a wrong calculation.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, how each presenter moves from a formula or a diagram back to a sentence about the real situation. Second, how sample spaces and the basic rules (complement, addition, multiplication) are used to justify a claim about fairness or risk. Third, where joint and conditional probabilities show up, and how the wording changes when you condition on information you already have.

About this video

Khan Academy works through an example of judging whether a procedure is fair by examining the probabilities involved. The focus is on checking whether each party or outcome has an equal chance, which requires laying out the possible outcomes and comparing their likelihoods. It is a compact, worked example rather than a lecture on theory.

Why this video

This is a direct demonstration of applying probability to inform a judgment about a real situation, and it shows how grounding your interpretation in the full set of outcomes is what makes the conclusion defensible.

About this video

Primer builds up the binomial distribution slowly, using animation and simulated trials rather than starting from the formula. Expect a careful look at how repeated independent trials generate a distribution of outcomes, and how counting arrangements of successes and failures connects to the multiplication and addition rules. Because the video runs simulations, it also illustrates how observed frequencies behave relative to theoretical values.

Why this video

It is the clearest video here for seeing the theoretical-versus-experimental distinction in action, and its visual counting of outcome sequences reinforces how sample spaces underpin any probability you later interpret.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos reinforce the module outcomes from the interpretation side. The Study Hall episode and the Primer video both make the structure of a sample space visible — through Venn diagrams and through enumerated sequences of trials — supporting MO1, while their treatment of unions, complements, and repeated independent events supports MO2 and MO5 by showing when each rule applies. Primer's simulations give a concrete feel for how experimental frequencies relate to theoretical values and the Law of Large Numbers (MO3). The Khan Academy fairness example and the decision-oriented framing in Study Hall model MO4 most directly: each ends not with a number but with a plain-language statement about what that number means for the choice being made.
