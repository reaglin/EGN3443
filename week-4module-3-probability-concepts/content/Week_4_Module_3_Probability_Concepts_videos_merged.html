Introduction to Probability

Introduction

Probability is the mathematical language we use to describe uncertainty. In engineering, almost nothing is known with perfect certainty: material strengths vary, sensors drift, components fail at unpredictable times, and measurements carry error. Probability gives us a disciplined way to quantify that uncertainty on a fixed scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), so that vague statements like "this part will probably hold" can be replaced by numbers that support design decisions and risk analysis.

This week you are building the vocabulary that the rest of the course depends on. Terms such as experiment, outcome, sample space, and event have precise technical meanings, and the rules you will learn later (complement, addition, multiplication) are stated in that vocabulary. You will also meet the distinction between theoretical probability, derived from the structure of the experiment, and experimental (empirical) probability, estimated from observed data — a distinction that matters any time you compare a model to real test results.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running glossary. Note how each presenter defines the sample space and how they list or diagram outcomes, watch for how probabilities are interpreted in words rather than just computed, and pay attention to any discussion of relative frequency and what happens as the number of trials grows. Where the videos overlap, compare notation; where they differ in emphasis, ask yourself which framing is most useful for an engineering problem.

Sample Spaces and Events

Introduction

Every probability problem starts with the same question: what could happen? The sample space is the complete list of possible outcomes of an experiment, and an event is any subset of that list — the outcomes we actually care about. Before you can assign a number to "the probability that a sampled part is defective" or "the probability that at least one of three sensors fails," you have to be able to write down the outcomes clearly and without gaps or overlaps.

In this course, sample spaces and events are the structural foundation for everything that follows: counting rules, the addition and multiplication rules, conditional probability, and eventually random variables and distributions. Most errors in probability problems are not arithmetic errors — they come from a poorly defined sample space, from double-counting outcomes, or from assuming outcomes are equally likely when they are not. Learning to build sample spaces systematically, using lists, tables, and tree diagrams, protects you from those mistakes.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, notice how each presenter decides what counts as a single outcome (an ordered pair? an unordered set? a sequence?), because that choice determines the whole sample space. Second, track the set language — union, intersection, complement, and disjoint (mutually exclusive) events — and how it maps onto plain-English phrases like "and," "or," and "not." Third, watch for when the equally likely assumption is used to compute a probability as a ratio of counts, and ask yourself whether that assumption would hold in an engineering setting.

Theoretical vs. Experimental Probability

Introduction

Probability can be approached from two directions. Theoretical probability is what you reason out in advance: if a process has equally likely outcomes, you count the outcomes favorable to an event and divide by the total number in the sample space. Experimental (or empirical) probability is what you measure: you run or observe trials and divide the number of times the event actually occurred by the number of trials performed. Both produce a number between 0 and 1, but they are obtained in fundamentally different ways and they carry different assumptions.

This distinction matters in engineering because many real systems do not come with a tidy list of equally likely outcomes. You rarely know the true failure probability of a component from first principles, so you estimate it from test data or field observations. Conversely, when a model is available, theoretical probability lets you predict behavior before building anything. The bridge between the two is the Law of Large Numbers, which says that as the number of independent trials grows, the relative frequency of an event tends to settle near its true probability. That is why a short run of 10 coin flips can look lopsided while 10,000 flips look nearly even.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, how is each probability computed — by counting outcomes in a sample space, or by counting occurrences in data? Second, what does the Law of Large Numbers actually promise, and what does it not promise (it does not say that a run of bad luck must be "balanced out")? Third, when experimental results disagree with theoretical predictions, what are the plausible explanations: too few trials, a biased or unfair process, or a flawed model assumption? Being able to argue which explanation applies is a core engineering skill.

Core Probability Rules

Introduction

Probability rules are the grammar of uncertainty. Once you can describe a sample space, you need a reliable set of operations for combining events: what happens when you want the chance that an event does not occur, the chance that at least one of two events occurs, or the chance that two events both occur. The core rules — complementary probability, the addition rule (for mutually exclusive events and the general form that subtracts the overlap), the multiplication rule (for independent events and the general form that uses a conditional probability), and conditional probability itself — cover nearly every basic computation you will do this semester.

In engineering, these rules show up constantly: the probability a redundant system fails only if every component fails, the probability a part is out of tolerance on at least one dimension, the probability a test flags a defect given that the part is actually defective. Getting the right answer usually depends less on arithmetic than on correctly identifying which rule applies and whether the events overlap or influence each other.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, are the events mutually exclusive (can they happen together)? Second, are they independent (does knowing one occurred change the probability of the other)? Third, when a problem has several steps, which rule applies at each step and how do the pieces combine? Try to pause each video before the solution is shown and set up the calculation yourself.

Interpreting and Applying Probability in Context

Introduction

A probability is a number, but a number by itself rarely helps anyone make a decision. This topic is about the step that comes after the arithmetic: saying clearly what a probability of 0.03, 0.5, or 0.97 actually means for the situation in front of you. That includes describing likelihood in plain language, explaining what the complement tells you, and being careful about whether a value came from theory (counting equally likely outcomes in a sample space) or from experiment (observing relative frequencies over many trials).

This matters in EGN3443 because engineers are usually not the final decision-maker acting alone. You will report reliability estimates, failure chances, and test results to managers, clients, regulators, and the public. A well-calculated probability that is poorly communicated — or that is quietly conditional on something the audience does not know about — can lead to a bad decision just as easily as a wrong calculation.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, how each presenter moves from a formula or a diagram back to a sentence about the real situation. Second, how sample spaces and the basic rules (complement, addition, multiplication) are used to justify a claim about fairness or risk. Third, where joint and conditional probabilities show up, and how the wording changes when you condition on information you already have.
