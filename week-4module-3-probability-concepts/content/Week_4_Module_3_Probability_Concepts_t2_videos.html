Introduction

Every probability problem starts with the same question: what could happen? The sample space is the complete list of possible outcomes of an experiment, and an event is any subset of that list — the outcomes we actually care about. Before you can assign a number to "the probability that a sampled part is defective" or "the probability that at least one of three sensors fails," you have to be able to write down the outcomes clearly and without gaps or overlaps.

In this course, sample spaces and events are the structural foundation for everything that follows: counting rules, the addition and multiplication rules, conditional probability, and eventually random variables and distributions. Most errors in probability problems are not arithmetic errors — they come from a poorly defined sample space, from double-counting outcomes, or from assuming outcomes are equally likely when they are not. Learning to build sample spaces systematically, using lists, tables, and tree diagrams, protects you from those mistakes.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, notice how each presenter decides what counts as a single outcome (an ordered pair? an unordered set? a sequence?), because that choice determines the whole sample space. Second, track the set language — union, intersection, complement, and disjoint (mutually exclusive) events — and how it maps onto plain-English phrases like "and," "or," and "not." Third, watch for when the equally likely assumption is used to compute a probability as a ratio of counts, and ask yourself whether that assumption would hold in an engineering setting.
