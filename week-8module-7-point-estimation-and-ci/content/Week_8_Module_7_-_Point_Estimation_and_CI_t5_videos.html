Introduction

Once you can compute a confidence interval, the harder and more important skill is saying what it means. A confidence level such as 95% describes the long-run performance of the procedure: if you repeated the sampling and interval construction many times, about 95% of the intervals produced would contain the true population parameter. It does not describe the probability that the one interval you happen to have in front of you captured the parameter, because that interval either does or does not contain it.

This distinction matters in engineering practice because confidence intervals are how you report uncertainty to people who will make decisions with your numbers. A wide interval signals an imprecise estimate and may mean you need a larger sample or better measurement; a narrow interval signals precision but still says nothing about whether the effect is large enough to matter practically. Learning the correct phrasing protects you from overstating what your data support.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the exact wording each presenter uses to state an interpretation, the misconceptions they flag and why those statements are wrong, and how interval width is connected to sample size, variability, and confidence level. Try restating each example interval in your own words before the narrator does, then compare.
