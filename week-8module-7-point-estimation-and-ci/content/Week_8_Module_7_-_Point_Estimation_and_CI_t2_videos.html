Introduction

Every time you take a sample and compute a statistic such as the sample mean, you get a number that depends on which items happened to land in your sample. Take a different sample and you get a different number. The sampling distribution is the probability distribution of that statistic across all possible samples of a given size, and it is the bridge between the probability tools you learned earlier in the course and the inference tools you are about to learn. Without it, a sample mean is just a number; with it, you can attach a standard error, a probability statement, and eventually a confidence interval.

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is the reason this works so broadly. It says that for a sufficiently large sample size, the distribution of the sample mean is approximately normal, centered at the population mean, with standard deviation sigma/sqrt(n) — and remarkably, this holds even when the population itself is skewed, discrete, or otherwise non-normal. That single result is what justifies the z-based and t-based methods you will use for the rest of this module.

As you watch, keep three questions in front of you. First, what exactly is random here — the individual measurement or the statistic? Second, how do the center and spread of the sampling distribution relate to the population mean and standard deviation, and why does the standard error shrink like 1/sqrt(n) rather than 1/n? Third, what changes when the population is more variable, or when n is small and the population is strongly non-normal? Try to finish with a working rule for when you are comfortable calling the sampling distribution approximately normal.
