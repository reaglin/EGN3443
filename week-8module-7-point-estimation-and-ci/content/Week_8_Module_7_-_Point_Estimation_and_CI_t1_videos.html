Introduction

Engineers almost never know the true value of a population parameter. We do not know the true mean tensile strength of a production lot of steel rods, or the true proportion of circuit boards that fail inspection. What we do have is a sample, and from that sample we compute a single number intended to stand in for the unknown parameter. That single number is a point estimate, and the formula or rule that produces it is called an estimator.

This topic builds the vocabulary and the judgment behind that process. You will see the sample mean used as a point estimator of the population mean and the sample proportion used as a point estimator of the population proportion. More importantly, you will see that more than one estimator can be proposed for the same parameter, which raises the real question of the week: what makes one estimator better than another? The standard answers are unbiasedness (on average, across repeated samples, the estimator hits the target), efficiency (among unbiased estimators, the one with the smaller variance is preferred), and related ideas such as consistency and sufficiency.

As you watch, keep the distinction between an estimator and an estimate clear in your mind: the estimator is a random variable with its own sampling distribution, while the estimate is the one number you got from your data. Watch for how each property is stated in terms of that sampling distribution — expected value for unbiasedness, variance for efficiency. These ideas set up the rest of the module, because a confidence interval is simply a point estimate surrounded by a margin of error, and the quality of the interval depends on the quality of the estimator at its center.

Video 2: Relative Efficiency of Estimators Example

Bill Kinney · 16:41 · 7,722 views · 4y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

Bill Kinney works a concrete example comparing two unbiased estimators of the same parameter — ((n+1)/n) times the sample maximum and twice the sample mean — and computes their relative efficiency ratio. The point of the example is that being unbiased is not enough, since two unbiased estimators can have very different variances. Expect to see variances derived and compared to decide which estimator is preferable.

Why this video

This video makes the unbiasedness-versus-efficiency distinction concrete and quantitative, which is exactly the comparison this topic asks you to be able to make; it also shows that the sample mean is not automatically the only candidate estimator.
