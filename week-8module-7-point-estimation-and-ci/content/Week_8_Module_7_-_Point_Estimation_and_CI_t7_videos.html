Introduction

Up to this point you have learned the machinery of estimation: how to compute a point estimate, how the Central Limit Theorem justifies normal-based inference, and how to assemble a confidence interval from a sample statistic, a critical value, and a standard error. This topic is about putting that machinery to work on real data. In practice, nobody hands you the formula to use — you have to decide what parameter you are estimating, which estimator fits the context, whether z or t is appropriate, and what the resulting interval actually tells you about the engineering question you started with.

The emphasis here is on judgment and interpretation. A confidence interval is a statement about precision and reliability, so you will practice reading interval width as evidence: a narrow interval signals a precise estimate, a wide one warns you that the sample is small or the process is highly variable, and whether a meaningful value (a specification limit, a target, or zero) falls inside the interval often drives the engineering conclusion. You will also practice stating conclusions in correct statistical language, avoiding the common error of claiming a probability statement about a fixed population parameter.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how each example identifies the parameter and estimator before computing anything, how the margin of error is built and what inputs control its size, and how the speaker words the final interpretation. The third video extends the same logic to regression, where the parameters being estimated are a slope and an intercept — a useful reminder that confidence intervals apply to any estimated quantity, not just means and proportions.
