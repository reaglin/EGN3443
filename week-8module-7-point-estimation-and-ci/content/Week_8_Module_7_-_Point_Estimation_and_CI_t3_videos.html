Introduction

A point estimate like the sample mean gives a single best guess for an unknown population mean, but it says nothing about how much that guess might be off. A confidence interval fixes this by reporting a range of plausible values built around the estimate. Every confidence interval for a mean shares the same structure: point estimate plus or minus a critical value times the standard error of the mean. Once you recognize that structure, the only real decisions left are which critical value to use and how to compute the standard error.

The choice of critical value depends on what you know. When the population standard deviation sigma is known (and the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal, by the Central Limit Theorem), you use a z critical value. When sigma is unknown and you must substitute the sample standard deviation s, the extra uncertainty is accounted for by the Student t distribution with n - 1 degrees of freedom. In engineering practice sigma is almost never known, so the t procedure is the workhorse method.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how each presenter identifies which distribution applies, how they read the correct critical value from a table or software (watch the degrees of freedom and the alpha/2 split carefully), and how the margin of error responds to changes in confidence level, sample size, and variability. Also notice the language used to interpret the final interval — the confidence level describes the long-run performance of the procedure, not the probability that a particular interval contains the mean.
