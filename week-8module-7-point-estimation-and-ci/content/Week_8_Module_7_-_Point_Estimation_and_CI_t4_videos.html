Introduction

Many engineering questions are about categories rather than measurements: What fraction of welds pass inspection? What proportion of users click a redesigned interface? What share of components fail within 1,000 hours? When the data are counts of successes out of n trials, the parameter of interest is a population proportion p, and the natural point estimate is the sample proportion p-hat = x/n. This topic extends the confidence interval machinery you built for means to this categorical setting.

The logic is the same as before, but the details differ. Because p-hat is an average of 0s and 1s, the Central Limit Theorem applies when the sample is large enough, which is usually checked with a success-failure condition (roughly np-hat and n(1 - p-hat) both at least 10 or 15) along with an assumption of independent observations. The standard error is computed from p-hat itself, so the interval takes the form p-hat plus or minus z times the square root of p-hat(1 - p-hat)/n. Note that we use a z critical value here, not a t value, because no separate sample standard deviation is being estimated.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: where the critical value comes from for a given confidence level, how the margin of error is assembled piece by piece, and how the final interval is stated in words. Correct interpretation matters as much as correct arithmetic. The confidence level describes the long-run reliability of the procedure, not the probability that one particular interval contains p. Also watch for how sample size and confidence level push the margin of error up or down, since that trade-off shows up in nearly every applied problem you will solve.
