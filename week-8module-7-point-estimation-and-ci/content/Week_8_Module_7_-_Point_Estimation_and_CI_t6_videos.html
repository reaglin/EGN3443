Introduction

A confidence interval is only as useful as it is precise. The margin of error — the quantity you add to and subtract from a point estimate — is what sets the width of the interval, and it is driven by three things: the confidence level you choose, the size of your sample, and how variable the population is. Raising the confidence level widens the interval; increasing the sample size narrows it (but only in proportion to the square root of n); and a more variable population forces a wider interval for the same sample size.

This matters in engineering practice because data collection costs money and time. If a client asks for an estimate of a mean strength or a proportion defective "within 2 units" or "within 3 percentage points," you need to translate that requirement into a required sample size before testing begins. That calculation is simply the margin-of-error formula solved for n, and it forces you to commit to a confidence level and to supply a planning value for the standard deviation or proportion.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: which pieces of the margin-of-error formula change when the confidence level changes, how much extra sample is needed to cut the margin of error in half, and how a required sample size is rounded (always up) once computed. Also notice the language used to interpret the finished interval — precision claims should always be stated in the units of the problem and tied to the stated confidence level.
