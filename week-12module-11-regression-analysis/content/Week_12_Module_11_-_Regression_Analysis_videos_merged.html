Foundations of Regression Analysis

Introduction

Regression analysis is the set of tools engineers use to describe and quantify how one variable responds to one or more others. In its simplest form, you fit a straight line through a cloud of data points so that the line summarizes the relationship between an independent (predictor) variable and a dependent (response) variable. Once that line exists, it can be used for two distinct jobs: explaining how much the response changes when a predictor changes, and predicting the response for conditions you have not yet observed.

This topic matters because most engineering decisions involve more than one influence at a time. Yield depends on temperature and pressure; cycle time depends on batch size, operator experience, and machine age. Moving from simple regression (one predictor) to multiple regression (several predictors) lets you separate these effects. Along the way you need a way to judge whether the model is any good, which is where model fit measures such as R-squared, the standard error, and the F-test come in, and whether the model is trustworthy, which is where the underlying assumptions (linearity, independence, constant variance, and approximately normal errors) come in.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, what exactly is being predicted and what is doing the predicting? Second, what number in the output tells you how well the model fits, and what does that number not tell you? Third, how does the mathematics scale up when you go from one predictor to many? The videos move roughly in that order, from conceptual framing to reading output to the underlying matrix machinery.

Simple Linear Regression

Introduction

Simple linear regression is the technique we use to describe the straight-line relationship between one predictor variable (x) and one response variable (y). In engineering practice, this is how you turn a scatter of measured data — load versus deflection, temperature versus reaction rate, flow rate versus pressure drop — into an equation you can use to explain behavior and predict new outcomes. The model has the familiar form y-hat = b0 + b1*x, where b0 is the intercept and b1 is the slope.

The core computational idea is the least squares method: among all possible lines, we choose the one that minimizes the sum of squared vertical distances (residuals) between the observed data points and the line. From that fitted line we then ask three separate questions. First, what do the coefficients mean physically — what does a one-unit change in x do to y, and is the intercept meaningful within the range of the data? Second, how well does the line fit — this is where R-squared, the proportion of variation in y explained by x, comes in. Third, is the relationship statistically real — this is the t-test on the slope (equivalently the F-test for the overall model) and its p-value.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running list of the formulas and what each quantity represents: the slope and intercept estimators, SST/SSR/SSE, R-squared, and the standard error. Pay attention to how each presenter moves from raw data to a fitted equation, and note the warning about extrapolating predictions outside the range of x values actually observed.

Multiple Regression Models

Introduction

Multiple regression extends the simple linear model by allowing two or more predictor variables to explain variation in a single response. Instead of fitting a line through a scatterplot, you fit a surface described by an equation of the form y = b0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + ... + bkxk, where each slope coefficient describes the effect of one predictor while the other predictors are held constant. That "holding the others constant" idea is the single most important conceptual shift in this module, and it is also the source of most misinterpretation.

For engineers, multiple regression is the everyday tool for modeling outcomes that depend on several inputs at once: strength as a function of mix proportions and curing time, energy use as a function of temperature and occupancy, process yield as a function of several operating settings. Along with building the model, you need tools for judging it: R-squared and adjusted R-squared for overall fit, t-tests and p-values for individual coefficients, the F-test for the model as a whole, and diagnostics such as the variance inflation factor (VIF) when predictors are correlated with each other.

As you watch the videos below, focus on how each piece of software output maps to a decision. Ask yourself: what does this coefficient mean in the units of the problem? Why does adjusted R-squared behave differently from R-squared when a predictor is added? And when two predictors carry overlapping information, how does multicollinearity distort the coefficient estimates and their standard errors? Keep a worked example of your own nearby so you can follow along with the reasoning rather than just the arithmetic.

Evaluating Regression Model Performance

Introduction

Fitting a regression line is only the first step. Once you have an equation, you have to ask harder questions: How much of the variation in the response does the model actually explain? Are the individual predictors contributing anything beyond chance? Does the model as a whole do better than simply predicting the mean? And, just as important, are the assumptions behind the least squares procedure reasonable for this data set? This topic gathers the standard tools engineers use to answer those questions — R-squared and adjusted R-squared, MSE and RMSE, coefficient p-values, the overall F-statistic, and residual diagnostics.

These ideas matter in this course because a regression model that looks good on a summary line can still be wrong in ways that cause bad engineering decisions. A high R-squared does not guarantee linearity, constant variance, or independent errors; adding predictors always raises R-squared, which is why adjusted R-squared exists; and residual plots often reveal curvature or funnel shapes that no single number will show you. Understanding the decomposition of total variability into explained and unexplained parts ties all of these metrics together, since R-squared, MSE, and the F-statistic are all built from sums of squares.

As you watch, keep track of three things. First, the vocabulary and arithmetic of sums of squares (total, regression/explained, error/residual) and how each metric is computed from them. Second, what a "good" residual plot looks like versus plots that signal a violated assumption. Third, the distinction between a model that fits the data you have and a model that will predict well on new data — that distinction motivates adjusted R-squared and RMSE as honest measures of error in the units of the response.

Interpreting Regression Results

Introduction

Fitting a regression line is only half the job. The other half is reading the output: what each coefficient means in the units of the problem, whether a predictor is statistically distinguishable from zero, how much of the variation in the response the model actually explains, and whether the model as a whole is doing anything useful. Statistical software will happily print a table of numbers for any dataset you hand it, so the engineer's value lies in interpretation, not computation.

In this course, regression output is the bridge between data and decisions. A slope coefficient tells you the expected change in the response per one-unit change in a predictor, holding the other predictors fixed; a p-value tells you how strong the evidence is against the hypothesis that the true coefficient is zero; R-squared and Adjusted R-squared describe how closely the fitted values track the observed data; and the F-test asks whether the predictors, taken together, explain more than chance would. Each piece answers a different question, and confusing them is the most common error students make.

As you watch the videos below, keep a blank regression output table in front of you and label the parts: coefficient, standard error, t statistic, p-value, confidence interval, SSR/SSE/SST, degrees of freedom, mean squares, F, R-squared, and standard error of the estimate. Ask yourself after each number: what practical statement about the engineering system could I write using this value, and what statement would be an overreach? Remember throughout that statistical significance is not the same as practical importance, that a high R-squared does not prove causation or guarantee valid assumptions, and that extrapolating beyond the range of your data is risky.

Applying Regression to Real-World Datasets

Introduction

So far in this module you have worked with regression in a fairly controlled setting: a clean dataset, a clear predictor, and a tidy output table. Real engineering data rarely arrives that way. This topic walks through the full applied workflow — choosing and cleaning a dataset, deciding which variables matter, framing a question the data can actually answer, fitting the model, reading the output in the context of the problem, checking whether the assumptions hold, refining the specification, and finally explaining what you found to someone who does not read regression tables.

This matters because almost every engineering role that uses statistics uses it this way. A coefficient only becomes useful when you can say what a one-unit change means in physical or financial terms, and a model only becomes trustworthy when you have looked at the residuals and admitted where it falls short. The judgment calls — which predictors to keep, whether an R-squared of 0.62 is good enough, whether an outlier is an error or a real signal — are the part that cannot be memorized.

As you watch, pay attention to the decisions being made rather than just the commands being typed. Notice how each presenter moves from data to a fitted model to a statement about the world, what evidence they use to justify keeping or dropping a variable, and how they describe uncertainty. Try to follow along in MATLAB, Python, or whatever tool your section uses, and ask yourself at each step how you would explain that step to a project manager.
