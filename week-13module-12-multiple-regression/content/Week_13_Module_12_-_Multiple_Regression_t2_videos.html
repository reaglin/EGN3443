Introduction

Multiple regression extends the simple linear model y = b0 + b1x + e by allowing several predictors to act on the response at once. Instead of asking how one variable relates to an outcome, we ask how a set of variables jointly explains it, and how much each one contributes once the others are accounted for. Model specification is the step where you decide which predictors belong in the equation, what form they take (continuous, categorical/indicator, possibly transformed), and what assumptions you are making about the error term.

This matters in engineering practice because real systems rarely depend on a single input. Yield depends on temperature and pressure and feed rate; travel time depends on distance, time of day, and road type. A badly specified model, one missing an important variable or padded with irrelevant ones, produces coefficients that are misleading even when the software reports impressive-looking output. Getting the specification right is what makes the later inference (t-tests on coefficients, the overall F-test, R-squared versus adjusted R-squared) meaningful.

As you watch, pay attention to three things. First, the language used to interpret a coefficient: a partial slope is the expected change in the response per unit change in that predictor while the other predictors are held fixed. Second, how predictors get chosen and compared, and why adding variables always raises R-squared but not necessarily adjusted R-squared. Third, how non-numeric predictors are brought into a model that is, mathematically, still linear in its coefficients.
