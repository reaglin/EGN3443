Introduction

In simple linear regression, the slope answers a straightforward question: when x goes up by one unit, how much does the predicted y change? In multiple regression we estimate several slopes at once, and each one is a partial regression coefficient. A partial coefficient tells you the expected change in the response for a one-unit increase in that predictor while all other predictors in the model are held fixed. That small phrase, "holding the others constant," is what makes multiple regression powerful and also what makes it easy to misread.

This matters in engineering work because real systems rarely depend on one input. Material strength, process yield, energy consumption, and travel time all respond to several factors simultaneously, and those factors are usually correlated with each other. Learning to read a coefficient as a unique contribution, net of the other predictors, is what lets you say something defensible about an individual factor instead of just quoting an overall fit statistic. You also need to recognize what the intercept means (and when it is meaningless), what the sign of a coefficient does and does not imply, and why coefficients measured in different units cannot be ranked by size alone.

As you watch, keep a few questions in front of you. What exactly is being held constant in each interpretation sentence? What are the units of the response and of each predictor, and does the stated interpretation carry those units? Does the intercept correspond to a physically possible setting of the predictors? And watch how each presenter phrases a conclusion out loud, because wording is where most interpretation errors show up on exams and in reports. Try writing your own one-sentence interpretation of a coefficient before the video gives one, then compare.

These three videos sit squarely on MO2, the interpretation of partial regression coefficients as the unique effect of a predictor with the others held constant.
