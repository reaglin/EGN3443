Introduction

Simple linear regression models an outcome using a single predictor, and it works well when one variable really does carry most of the explanatory weight. In engineering practice, that is rarely the case: fuel consumption depends on more than speed, concrete strength depends on more than cure time, and the price of a used car depends on more than mileage. When important variables are left out, the slope you estimate for the one predictor you kept can absorb the influence of everything you ignored, and your predictions and conclusions suffer.

Multiple regression extends the same least-squares idea to two or more predictors at once. The structure is familiar — an intercept, a slope for each predictor, and residuals — but the interpretation changes in an important way: each coefficient now describes the effect of its own predictor while the other predictors are held constant. That idea, often called controlling for other variables, is the conceptual bridge between what you learned last week and what you will do for the rest of this module.

As you watch these videos, focus on three things: how the simple regression equation is generalized to several predictors, what the phrase "holding the other variables constant" actually means for a fitted coefficient, and what signals tell you that one predictor is not enough. Keep notes on the vocabulary (predictor, response, coefficient, controlling for), because the later topics on R-squared, adjusted R-squared, and the F-test all build directly on it.
