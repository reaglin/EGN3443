From Simple to Multiple Regression

Introduction

Simple linear regression models an outcome using a single predictor, and it works well when one variable really does carry most of the explanatory weight. In engineering practice, that is rarely the case: fuel consumption depends on more than speed, concrete strength depends on more than cure time, and the price of a used car depends on more than mileage. When important variables are left out, the slope you estimate for the one predictor you kept can absorb the influence of everything you ignored, and your predictions and conclusions suffer.

Multiple regression extends the same least-squares idea to two or more predictors at once. The structure is familiar — an intercept, a slope for each predictor, and residuals — but the interpretation changes in an important way: each coefficient now describes the effect of its own predictor while the other predictors are held constant. That idea, often called controlling for other variables, is the conceptual bridge between what you learned last week and what you will do for the rest of this module.

As you watch these videos, focus on three things: how the simple regression equation is generalized to several predictors, what the phrase "holding the other variables constant" actually means for a fitted coefficient, and what signals tell you that one predictor is not enough. Keep notes on the vocabulary (predictor, response, coefficient, controlling for), because the later topics on R-squared, adjusted R-squared, and the F-test all build directly on it.

Model Specification and Structure

Introduction

Multiple regression extends the simple linear model y = b0 + b1x + e by allowing several predictors to act on the response at once. Instead of asking how one variable relates to an outcome, we ask how a set of variables jointly explains it, and how much each one contributes once the others are accounted for. Model specification is the step where you decide which predictors belong in the equation, what form they take (continuous, categorical/indicator, possibly transformed), and what assumptions you are making about the error term.

This matters in engineering practice because real systems rarely depend on a single input. Yield depends on temperature and pressure and feed rate; travel time depends on distance, time of day, and road type. A badly specified model, one missing an important variable or padded with irrelevant ones, produces coefficients that are misleading even when the software reports impressive-looking output. Getting the specification right is what makes the later inference (t-tests on coefficients, the overall F-test, R-squared versus adjusted R-squared) meaningful.

As you watch, pay attention to three things. First, the language used to interpret a coefficient: a partial slope is the expected change in the response per unit change in that predictor while the other predictors are held fixed. Second, how predictors get chosen and compared, and why adding variables always raises R-squared but not necessarily adjusted R-squared. Third, how non-numeric predictors are brought into a model that is, mathematically, still linear in its coefficients.

Interpreting Regression Coefficients

Introduction

In simple linear regression, the slope answers a straightforward question: when x goes up by one unit, how much does the predicted y change? In multiple regression we estimate several slopes at once, and each one is a partial regression coefficient. A partial coefficient tells you the expected change in the response for a one-unit increase in that predictor while all other predictors in the model are held fixed. That small phrase, "holding the others constant," is what makes multiple regression powerful and also what makes it easy to misread.

This matters in engineering work because real systems rarely depend on one input. Material strength, process yield, energy consumption, and travel time all respond to several factors simultaneously, and those factors are usually correlated with each other. Learning to read a coefficient as a unique contribution, net of the other predictors, is what lets you say something defensible about an individual factor instead of just quoting an overall fit statistic. You also need to recognize what the intercept means (and when it is meaningless), what the sign of a coefficient does and does not imply, and why coefficients measured in different units cannot be ranked by size alone.

As you watch, keep a few questions in front of you. What exactly is being held constant in each interpretation sentence? What are the units of the response and of each predictor, and does the stated interpretation carry those units? Does the intercept correspond to a physically possible setting of the predictors? And watch how each presenter phrases a conclusion out loud, because wording is where most interpretation errors show up on exams and in reports. Try writing your own one-sentence interpretation of a coefficient before the video gives one, then compare.

Assessing Model Fit

Introduction

Once you have fit a multiple regression model, the next question is how well it actually describes the data. Measures of model fit summarize how much of the variation in the response variable the model accounts for, and they give you a basis for comparing competing models built from different sets of predictors. In this topic we focus on the coefficient of determination, R-squared, and its companion statistic, adjusted R-squared.

R-squared is easy to compute and easy to over-trust. A key fact in multiple regression is that R-squared can never decrease when you add another predictor, even a useless one, so a high R-squared alone does not mean you have a good or parsimonious model. Adjusted R-squared addresses this by penalizing the model for the number of predictors relative to the sample size, so it can go down when an added variable does not earn its place. Understanding the difference is what lets you use these numbers responsibly when refining a model.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: the formulas and where the degrees of freedom enter, the behavior of R-squared versus adjusted R-squared as predictors are added, and how these statistics appear alongside the ANOVA table, the F-test, and the standard error of the estimate in typical regression output. Try to connect each number on the output to the sums of squares it comes from.

Building and Evaluating Multiple Regression Models

Introduction

Multiple regression extends simple linear regression by allowing several predictor variables to explain variation in a single response variable. In engineering settings you rarely have just one input driving an outcome: material strength may depend on temperature, curing time, and composition all at once. The multiple regression model expresses the response as an intercept plus a weighted sum of predictors, where each coefficient is estimated by ordinary least squares and interpreted as the effect of that predictor while the others are held constant.

Building a usable model is more than running the computation. You need to judge how well the model fits (R-squared versus adjusted R-squared), whether the model as a whole explains a significant share of variance (the overall F-test), whether individual coefficients are significant (t-tests), and whether the residuals behave as the assumptions of linearity, constant variance, independence, and normality require. Models with too many weak predictors can look impressive on R-squared alone while adding little real explanatory value, which is why refinement strategies matter.

The videos below walk through these decisions with worked examples. As you watch, pay attention to the reasoning used to decide whether a variable earns its place in the model, how adjusted R-squared responds when predictors are added or dropped, and how the F-test statistic is formed from the regression and error sums of squares. Keep a note of the vocabulary used for output tables, since that is the form in which you will see most software results.
