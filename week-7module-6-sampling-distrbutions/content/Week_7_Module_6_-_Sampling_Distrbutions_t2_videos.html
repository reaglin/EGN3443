Introduction

Almost every statistical question an engineer asks is about a population — all the bolts coming off a production line, all the bridge welds in a fleet of structures, all the response times of a sensor design. Measuring every member of that population is usually impossible, so we measure a sample and compute numbers from it. The fixed, usually unknown numbers that describe the population (such as the population mean, the population standard deviation, or a population proportion) are called parameters. The numbers we actually compute from our sample data are called statistics, and they are our best available estimates of those parameters.

The distinction matters because parameters and statistics behave very differently. A parameter is a single fixed value; a statistic changes every time you draw a new sample. That variation is called sampling error, and it is not a mistake — it is the natural consequence of looking at part of a population instead of all of it. Understanding this difference is the foundation for everything else in this module: sampling distributions, standard error, and the Central Limit Theorem all exist to describe how far a statistic is likely to fall from the parameter it estimates.

As you watch the videos below, pay close attention to the notation conventions (Greek letters such as mu and sigma for parameters versus Roman letters such as x-bar, s, and p-hat for statistics), and to how each presenter identifies the population in a worded scenario before naming the parameter and statistic. Practice the habit of asking two questions of any data problem: what group am I trying to describe, and what did I actually measure?
