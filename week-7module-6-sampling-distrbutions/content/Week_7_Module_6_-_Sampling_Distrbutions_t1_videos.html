Introduction

Up to this point you have mostly worked with a single data set: you collect a sample, compute a mean or a proportion, and report it. This topic asks a different question — what would happen if you took another sample, and another, and another? Because every sample is a slightly different subset of the population, every sample statistic is a slightly different number. The pattern formed by those many possible values is called a sampling distribution, and it is the bridge between the data you actually have and the population you actually care about.

Two distinctions anchor the week. First, a population parameter (such as the true mean strength of a batch of components) is a fixed but usually unknown number, while a sample statistic (the mean of the 30 components you tested) is a random quantity that changes from sample to sample. Second, that randomness is not noise to be ignored — it is structured, predictable, and quantifiable. Its spread is the standard error, and it shrinks as sample size grows. Everything you do later with confidence intervals and hypothesis tests rests on knowing how much a statistic is expected to bounce around just by chance.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: (1) the vocabulary and notation used for parameters versus statistics, (2) the simulation or repeated-sampling demonstrations, which show the distribution being built up one sample at a time, and (3) any formulas given for the mean and standard deviation (standard error) of the sampling distribution. Try to keep straight which distribution is being described at any moment — the population, one sample, or the distribution of a statistic across many samples.

About this video

From Khan Academy's AP Statistics sequence, this video extends the idea to a statistic formed from two samples: the difference in sample means. Expect it to treat the difference as a random variable with its own sampling distribution, including how its center and spread relate to the two populations being compared.

Why this video

It shows that sampling distributions apply to any statistic, not just a single mean, and it previews the comparison problems where you must decide whether an observed difference is just sampling variability or something real.
