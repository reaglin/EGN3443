Introduction

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is the bridge between probability theory and practical statistical inference. It tells us that if we repeatedly draw random samples of size n from a population and compute the sample mean each time, the distribution of those sample means tends toward a normal distribution as n grows — even when the original population is skewed, uniform, or otherwise non-normal. The mean of that sampling distribution equals the population mean, and its standard deviation, called the standard error, equals the population standard deviation divided by the square root of n.

This result matters in EGN3443 because nearly every inference technique you will use later — confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, process control limits — rests on being able to treat the sample mean as approximately normal. It is also why engineers can quantify how much of an observed difference between two measurements is just sampling noise and how much is a real effect. The inverse square-root relationship between sample size and standard error is what tells you how many measurements you need to achieve a desired precision.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to four things: (1) the distinction between the population distribution and the sampling distribution of the mean, (2) the two formulas for the mean and standard error of that sampling distribution, (3) what changes visually as the sample size increases, and (4) the conditions — random sampling, independence, and either a normal population or a sufficiently large n — under which the normal approximation is justified.
