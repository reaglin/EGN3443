Introduction

A sampling distribution describes how a statistic — most often the sample mean — behaves across all the samples we could have drawn from a population. In earlier parts of this module you built that idea formally: the mean of the sampling distribution equals the population mean, the standard error equals sigma divided by the square root of n, and the Central Limit Theorem tells us the shape becomes approximately normal as n grows. This topic turns those formulas outward, toward the questions an engineer actually asks: Is this batch different from spec, or is it just sampling noise? How large a sample do I need before I trust the estimate?

Reading a sampling distribution well means keeping three distributions separate in your mind: the population, a single sample, and the distribution of the statistic. Mixing them up is the single most common source of error in inference. Once they are separate, probability statements about sample means become routine — standardize using the standard error rather than the population standard deviation, then read a normal probability — and those statements are the foundation for confidence intervals and hypothesis tests later in the course.

As you watch the videos below, track four things each time a worked example appears: what the population parameters are assumed to be, what the sample size is, how the standard error is computed, and whether the normal approximation is justified by a normal population or by a large enough n. Notice especially what happens to the spread when n changes — that inverse square-root relationship is what lets you use sample size as a design decision rather than an afterthought.
