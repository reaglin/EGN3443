Introduction

Every time you take a sample from a population, the sample mean you compute will be a little different from the one you would get with a different sample. The sampling distribution describes that variation, and its spread is measured by the standard error of the mean. Because the standard error equals the population standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size, it shrinks as n grows — but it shrinks slowly, following the square root rather than n itself.

This relationship matters in engineering practice. If you are estimating the mean tensile strength of a batch of parts, the mean flow rate in a process, or the mean response time of a system, the sample size you choose directly determines how precise your estimate is and how wide your confidence interval will be. Small samples produce estimates that bounce around a great deal; large samples produce tight, reliable estimates but cost more time, material, and money. Choosing n is therefore a trade-off, not a formality.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the exact formula for standard error and what each symbol means, how the spread of the sampling distribution visibly changes as n increases, and how that spread translates into margin of error when you move from describing a distribution to making an inference about a population parameter.
