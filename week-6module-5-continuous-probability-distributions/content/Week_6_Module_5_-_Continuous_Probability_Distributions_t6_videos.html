Introduction

Up to this point you have worked with continuous distributions mostly as formulas: a density function, an integral, a z-score table. This topic shifts the emphasis to judgement. Given a real data set or a described engineering situation — machining tolerances, time between equipment failures, arrival times, repair costs — which continuous model is defensible, and what does the resulting probability actually tell a decision-maker?

The practical skill has three parts. First, matching the physical behaviour of the process to the shape and assumptions of a candidate distribution: constant likelihood across an interval suggests uniform, memoryless waiting between independent events suggests exponential, the accumulation of many small independent effects suggests normal, and multiplicative effects often produce a right-skewed lognormal. Second, computing the probability correctly. Third, checking whether the assumption held at all, and reporting the result honestly, including its limitations.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to the reasoning that precedes each calculation: what feature of the scenario justified the choice of model? Note also where the presenters mention related distributions — the gamma and lognormal are not required computations in this module, but seeing them helps you understand why the uniform, normal, and exponential are chosen for some problems and not others.

Together these videos move beyond formula practice toward model selection and interpretation.
