Introduction to Continuous Probability Distributions

Introduction

Up to this point in the course you have worked mostly with discrete random variables, where a variable takes on countable values and each value carries its own probability. Continuous random variables behave differently: measurements like time to failure, shaft diameter, voltage, or wait time can fall anywhere within an interval, and no finite list can capture all the possible outcomes. That shift forces us to change how we describe probability itself.

The central idea of this topic is that for a continuous variable, the probability of any single exact value is zero, and probability is instead measured over intervals as the area under a probability density function (PDF). The cumulative distribution function (CDF) accumulates that area from the left, giving P(X less than or equal to x) directly, which makes it the practical tool for computing interval probabilities. Because the total area under a PDF must equal 1, and because the mean and variance of a continuous distribution are defined through integration rather than summation, these ideas set up everything you will do later with the uniform, normal, and exponential distributions.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, what exactly does the height of a PDF curve represent, and why is it not a probability? Second, how do the PDF and CDF relate to each other graphically — area versus accumulated area? Third, how do the familiar notions of expected value and variance carry over from the discrete case? Sketching the curves yourself while watching will make the area interpretation stick far better than passive viewing.

The Uniform Distribution

Introduction

The continuous uniform distribution is the simplest continuous probability model: every value in an interval [a, b] is equally likely, so the probability density function is a flat, rectangular curve of constant height 1/(b-a). Because the density is constant, probabilities become areas of rectangles, which means you can often compute them with basic geometry instead of calculus. This makes the uniform distribution an ideal starting point for Module 5, where you move from discrete random variables and probability mass functions to continuous random variables described by a PDF and a cumulative distribution function (CDF).

In engineering practice, the uniform distribution models situations where you know only the range of possible values and have no reason to favor one value over another: waiting time for a bus that arrives every 10 minutes, rounding or measurement error within a tolerance band, or random number generation in simulation studies. It is also the building block for Monte Carlo methods, where uniform random numbers are transformed into samples from other distributions. Knowing its mean (a+b)/2 and variance (b-a)^2/12 lets you summarize such scenarios quickly.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how the height of the density is determined from the endpoints a and b, how a probability statement such as P(X < c) or P(c < X < d) is translated into an area or into the CDF expression (x-a)/(b-a), and how conditional or "given that" problems shrink the interval you are working with. Try pausing each worked example, solving it yourself, and then comparing your setup to the one on screen.

The Normal Distribution

Introduction

The normal distribution — also called the Gaussian distribution or the bell curve — is the most widely used continuous probability model in engineering and science. It is completely described by two parameters: the mean, which locates the center of the symmetric bell, and the standard deviation, which controls how wide or narrow the curve is. Because the curve is a probability density function, probabilities correspond to areas under the curve rather than to heights at single points, so finding P(a < X < b) means finding the area between two values.

In this course, the normal distribution matters for two practical reasons. First, many measured quantities in engineering — machined dimensions, material strengths, measurement errors, process outputs — are approximately normal, so the model lets you predict how often a part falls outside tolerance or a process drifts out of spec. Second, the standardization step (the z-score, z = (x - mu)/sigma) converts any normal distribution into the single standard normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, which is what makes a one-page table or a calculator function usable for every problem you will meet.

As you watch the videos below, keep three things in your notes: how a z-score is computed and what it means in "number of standard deviations from the mean"; exactly what region of the curve the table you are using reports (mean-to-z versus cumulative from the left), since mixing these up is the most common source of errors; and how the Empirical Rule gives you a quick sanity check on any table-based answer. Sketching the bell curve and shading the region you want before you compute anything is the habit worth building.

The Exponential Distribution

Introduction

The exponential distribution is the continuous model engineers reach for when the quantity of interest is a waiting time: how long until the next arrival at a service counter, the next packet on a network, the next failure of a component, or the next decay event from a radioactive sample. It is defined by a single parameter, the rate lambda, and its density f(x) = lambda*e^(-lambda*x) for x greater than or equal to 0 decays smoothly from a maximum at zero, which is why small waiting times are more likely than long ones.

Because the exponential distribution has a closed-form cumulative distribution function, F(x) = 1 - e^(-lambda*x), you can compute probabilities directly by substitution instead of using a table, as you must for the normal distribution. Its mean and standard deviation are both 1/lambda, and it is the only continuous distribution with the memoryless property: the probability of waiting an additional t units does not depend on how long you have already waited. That property is both powerful and easy to misuse, so it deserves careful attention.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, how is lambda defined and what are its units relative to the units of the waiting time? Second, how does the exponential distribution arise as the time between events in a Poisson process, linking this week's continuous material back to the discrete distributions you studied earlier? Third, what does memorylessness imply about modeling component lifetimes, and when is it a reasonable assumption for real engineering hardware?

Calculating and Interpreting Probabilities

Introduction

In this topic you move from describing continuous distributions to actually computing with them. For a continuous random variable, probability is area under the density curve, so every calculation reduces to finding an area between two points, to the left of a point, or to the right of a point. For the uniform distribution that area is a rectangle you can find geometrically; for the exponential distribution it comes from a short closed-form cumulative distribution function; for the normal distribution it requires converting values to z-scores and then reading a table or using technology.

These skills matter because almost every applied problem in this course ends with a probability statement that someone has to act on: the chance a component lasts beyond its warranty period, the chance a measurement falls outside specification limits, the chance a wait time exceeds a service target. Getting the number right is only half the job; you also need to state clearly what the number means in context and what decision it supports.

While watching, pay close attention to three things. First, how each presenter sets up the problem: which parameters are identified, and how the question is translated into an area. Second, which table or technology convention is used, since normal tables differ (mean-to-z versus cumulative-from-the-left) and mixing them up is one of the most common errors. Third, the small habits that prevent mistakes: sketching the curve and shading the region, checking that your answer is between 0 and 1, and confirming that the answer is on the plausible side of 0.5 given where your cutoff sits relative to the mean.

Applying Continuous Distributions to Real-World Scenarios

Introduction

Up to this point you have worked with continuous distributions mostly as formulas: a density function, an integral, a z-score table. This topic shifts the emphasis to judgement. Given a real data set or a described engineering situation — machining tolerances, time between equipment failures, arrival times, repair costs — which continuous model is defensible, and what does the resulting probability actually tell a decision-maker?

The practical skill has three parts. First, matching the physical behaviour of the process to the shape and assumptions of a candidate distribution: constant likelihood across an interval suggests uniform, memoryless waiting between independent events suggests exponential, the accumulation of many small independent effects suggests normal, and multiplicative effects often produce a right-skewed lognormal. Second, computing the probability correctly. Third, checking whether the assumption held at all, and reporting the result honestly, including its limitations.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to the reasoning that precedes each calculation: what feature of the scenario justified the choice of model? Note also where the presenters mention related distributions — the gamma and lognormal are not required computations in this module, but seeing them helps you understand why the uniform, normal, and exponential are chosen for some problems and not others.
