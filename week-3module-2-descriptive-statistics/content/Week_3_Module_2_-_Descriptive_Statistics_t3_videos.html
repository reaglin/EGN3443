Introduction

Two datasets can share the same mean and still behave very differently. One batch of machined parts might cluster tightly around the target dimension while another swings widely above and below it, and that difference in spread is often what determines whether a design, a process, or a measurement system is acceptable. Measures of variability give us the vocabulary and the arithmetic to describe that spread precisely.

In this topic you will work with three standard measures: the range (the distance between the largest and smallest values), the variance (the average squared deviation from the mean), and the standard deviation (the square root of the variance, expressed in the same units as the original data). Along the way you will see why deviations from the mean are squared rather than simply added, how the mean absolute deviation offers an alternative approach, and when the simplicity of the range is adequate versus when it hides important information.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the step-by-step mechanics of each calculation, the units attached to each result, and the reasoning each presenter gives for preferring one measure over another. Try pausing to compute a small example yourself before the presenter reveals the answer, and keep asking what a given number would mean to an engineer looking at a real process.

This video walks through dispersion measures in sequence, beginning with the mean deviation, then mean absolute deviation, then variance and standard deviation. Why this video
