Introduction to Descriptive Statistics

Introduction

Descriptive statistics are the tools we use to take a raw collection of numbers and turn it into something a person can actually understand. Instead of staring at hundreds of measurements from a manufacturing line, a materials test, or a traffic survey, we summarise them: a typical value, a sense of how spread out the data are, and a picture of the overall shape of the distribution. That summary is almost always the first thing an engineer produces after collecting data, and it is often what ends up in the report that others read.

In EGN3443, this topic sets the foundation for everything that follows. Before you can reason about probability models, sampling, or inference, you need to be comfortable describing what a dataset actually looks like — its center, its variability, and its shape — and you need to know which summary is appropriate for which kind of data. Descriptive statistics also protect you from mistakes: a mean reported without any measure of spread, or applied to a strongly skewed dataset, can be badly misleading.

As you watch the videos below, look for three things. First, the standard categories of descriptive measures (central tendency, variability, and distribution shape) and the vocabulary used for each. Second, the reasoning behind choosing one measure over another for a particular dataset. Third, how descriptive statistics fit into a larger workflow — they are the starting point of analysis, the step that generates questions rather than final answers.

Measures of Central Tendency

Introduction

When engineers collect data — test results, failure times, sensor readings, survey responses — the first question is usually "what is a typical value?" Measures of central tendency answer that question. The mean is the arithmetic average, the median is the middle value once the data are ordered, and the mode is the value that occurs most often. Each summarizes the "center" of a dataset, but each does so differently, and they can disagree sharply.

This topic matters because almost everything that follows in the course builds on it. Variance and standard deviation are measured around the mean; the shape of a distribution is often described by how the mean, median, and mode relate to one another; and later work in probability and inference depends on knowing what a sample average represents. Just as importantly, choosing the wrong measure can distort a conclusion — a single extreme value can pull the mean far from anything typical, while the median barely moves.

As you watch, focus on three things: the exact computational procedure for each measure (especially the median with an even number of observations), how outliers and skew affect each measure differently, and the decision rules for choosing one measure over another based on data type (nominal, ordinal, interval/ratio) and distribution shape. Try pausing each video to compute the answer yourself before the presenter reveals it.

Measures of Variability

Introduction

Two datasets can share the same mean and still behave very differently. One batch of machined parts might cluster tightly around the target dimension while another swings widely above and below it, and that difference in spread is often what determines whether a design, a process, or a measurement system is acceptable. Measures of variability give us the vocabulary and the arithmetic to describe that spread precisely.

In this topic you will work with three standard measures: the range (the distance between the largest and smallest values), the variance (the average squared deviation from the mean), and the standard deviation (the square root of the variance, expressed in the same units as the original data). Along the way you will see why deviations from the mean are squared rather than simply added, how the mean absolute deviation offers an alternative approach, and when the simplicity of the range is adequate versus when it hides important information.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the step-by-step mechanics of each calculation, the units attached to each result, and the reasoning each presenter gives for preferring one measure over another. Try pausing to compute a small example yourself before the presenter reveals the answer, and keep asking what a given number would mean to an engineer looking at a real process.

Data Distribution

Introduction

A data distribution describes how the values in a dataset are spread across the range of possible outcomes — where observations cluster, where they thin out, and whether the pattern is balanced or lopsided. Before you compute a single summary number, it is worth looking at the shape of the data in a histogram or dot plot, because shape tells you a great deal about what the data are doing and which summaries will describe them honestly.

In this course, shape matters for practical reasons. A symmetrical distribution lets the mean sit comfortably at the centre, while a skewed distribution pulls the mean toward the long tail and can make it a misleading summary of a 'typical' value. Engineering data — component lifetimes, failure times, waiting times, measurement errors, concentrations — are frequently skewed, so recognising skew is part of reading your data correctly rather than an abstract exercise.

As you watch the videos below, focus on three things: the vocabulary (symmetric, uniform, bimodal, positively/right skewed, negatively/left skewed), the direction convention (the skew is named for the side with the long tail, not the side with the tall bars), and how the relative positions of mean, median and mode shift as the shape changes. Also pay attention to how each presenter talks about spread — the range of values covered and how tightly or loosely the data cluster — since that language will be useful when you describe real datasets later in the module.

Selecting and Applying Appropriate Statistical Measures

Introduction

Descriptive statistics are only useful when the measure fits the data. A mean computed on a heavily skewed cost dataset, or a "mode" reported for continuous sensor readings, can mislead an audience even when the arithmetic is flawless. This topic focuses on the decision that comes before the calculation: identifying whether data are nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio, checking the shape of the distribution and the presence of outliers, and then choosing the measure of central tendency and variability that honestly represents the data.

In an engineering context this matters because your numbers travel. A single summary statistic may end up in a design review, a quality report, or a recommendation to a client who will never see the raw data. Choosing well — and explaining why you chose it — is part of the engineering work, not an afterthought.

While watching the videos below, pay attention to three things: how each measure is actually computed, what each measure assumes about the data, and how the presenter decides which measure to report. Try to connect those decisions back to the data types and distribution shapes covered earlier in this module, and note any example where swapping one measure for another would change the story the data tells.
