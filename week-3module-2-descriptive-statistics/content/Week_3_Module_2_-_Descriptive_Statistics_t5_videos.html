Introduction

Descriptive statistics are only useful when the measure fits the data. A mean computed on a heavily skewed cost dataset, or a "mode" reported for continuous sensor readings, can mislead an audience even when the arithmetic is flawless. This topic focuses on the decision that comes before the calculation: identifying whether data are nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio, checking the shape of the distribution and the presence of outliers, and then choosing the measure of central tendency and variability that honestly represents the data.

In an engineering context this matters because your numbers travel. A single summary statistic may end up in a design review, a quality report, or a recommendation to a client who will never see the raw data. Choosing well — and explaining why you chose it — is part of the engineering work, not an afterthought.

While watching the videos below, pay attention to three things: how each measure is actually computed, what each measure assumes about the data, and how the presenter decides which measure to report. Try to connect those decisions back to the data types and distribution shapes covered earlier in this module, and note any example where swapping one measure for another would change the story the data tells.
