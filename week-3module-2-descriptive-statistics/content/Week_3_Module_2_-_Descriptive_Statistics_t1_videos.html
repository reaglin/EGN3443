Introduction

Descriptive statistics are the tools we use to take a raw collection of numbers and turn it into something a person can actually understand. Instead of staring at hundreds of measurements from a manufacturing line, a materials test, or a traffic survey, we summarise them: a typical value, a sense of how spread out the data are, and a picture of the overall shape of the distribution. That summary is almost always the first thing an engineer produces after collecting data, and it is often what ends up in the report that others read.

In EGN3443, this topic sets the foundation for everything that follows. Before you can reason about probability models, sampling, or inference, you need to be comfortable describing what a dataset actually looks like — its center, its variability, and its shape — and you need to know which summary is appropriate for which kind of data. Descriptive statistics also protect you from mistakes: a mean reported without any measure of spread, or applied to a strongly skewed dataset, can be badly misleading.

As you watch the videos below, look for three things. First, the standard categories of descriptive measures (central tendency, variability, and distribution shape) and the vocabulary used for each. Second, the reasoning behind choosing one measure over another for a particular dataset. Third, how descriptive statistics fit into a larger workflow — they are the starting point of analysis, the step that generates questions rather than final answers.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos build the conceptual groundwork the module outcomes depend on. The longer Art+Science video and the Learning Champion overview introduce the categories of descriptive measures — central tendency and variability — that you will compute by hand in MO1 and MO2, and they establish the vocabulary of distribution shape needed for MO3. The videos are conceptual rather than computational, so pair them with the module's worked problems and dataset exercises to practise the actual calculations.
