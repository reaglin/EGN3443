Introduction

Every hypothesis test ends with a decision — reject the null hypothesis or fail to reject it — and that decision can be wrong in two distinct ways. A Type I error (a false positive) happens when we reject a null hypothesis that is actually true; its probability is the significance level, alpha, that we choose before collecting data. A Type II error (a false negative) happens when we fail to reject a null hypothesis that is actually false; its probability is called beta, and the complement, 1 minus beta, is the power of the test.

This matters in engineering because the two errors usually carry very different costs. Falsely concluding that a new alloy is stronger, that a process change reduced defects, or that a contaminant level exceeds a limit leads to one kind of expensive mistake; failing to detect a real problem leads to another. Choosing alpha, sample size, and effect size is therefore an engineering judgment about which error you can least afford, not just a statistical formality.

As you watch, keep track of four things: the 2-by-2 table of truth versus decision, which error alpha controls and which beta controls, why lowering alpha tends to raise beta when sample size is fixed, and how increasing sample size (or effect size) raises power without that trade-off. Also notice the careful wording used for conclusions — we never "accept" the null hypothesis, we only fail to reject it.
