Introduction

A test statistic is a single number computed from your sample that measures how far the sample result sits from what the null hypothesis predicts, expressed in units of standard error. Because every test statistic is compared against a reference distribution — the standard normal curve for a z-test, a t-distribution with a particular number of degrees of freedom for a t-test — you cannot interpret the number without knowing which sampling distribution it belongs to. The sampling distribution is what turns an abstract distance into a probability statement, and that probability is the p-value.

In this course the practical skills are: identify what is known and unknown about the population (is sigma given, or only the sample standard deviation s?), pick the matching test statistic, compute it correctly, determine the degrees of freedom when using t, and convert the result into a p-value you can compare against alpha. These steps sit at the heart of nearly every engineering hypothesis test you will run, from verifying a manufacturing tolerance to checking a vendor's performance claim.

As you watch, keep three questions in front of you: What is being subtracted from what in the numerator? What goes in the denominator, and why is it a standard error rather than a standard deviation? And which curve is the resulting value being compared against? If you can answer those for each example, the formulas stop being things to memorize and become things you can reconstruct.
