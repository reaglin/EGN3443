Foundations of Hypothesis Testing

Introduction

Hypothesis testing is the formal procedure engineers use to decide whether sample data provide enough evidence to support a claim about a population. Instead of asking "is this value exactly right?", we ask a sharper question: "if the status quo were true, how surprising would our data be?" That shift in framing is the heart of this week's material, and it underlies everything that follows — test statistics, p-values, significance levels, and error types.

The core machinery is a pair of competing statements. The null hypothesis (H0) expresses no change, no difference, or no effect, and it is the statement we assume true while we evaluate the evidence. The alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha) expresses the claim we would need evidence to accept, and it may be directional (greater than, less than) or non-directional (not equal to). Learning to translate a real-world engineering claim — a supplier says the mean tensile strength is at least 450 MPa, a redesign is supposed to reduce cycle time — into this pair of symbolic statements is the first skill you must master.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how the speaker decides which statement gets the equality sign and becomes H0, how the wording of a claim determines whether the alternative is one-sided or two-sided, and how the logic of "reject H0" versus "fail to reject H0" is phrased. Note especially that we never "prove" the null hypothesis — we only decide whether the evidence is strong enough to abandon it.

Formulating Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Introduction

Hypothesis testing begins long before any arithmetic: it begins with a careful translation of a claim into two competing statements about a population parameter. The null hypothesis (H0) is the statement of "no effect," "no difference," or "the claim as stated," and it always contains an equality. The alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha) is the statement you are gathering evidence for, and it is written with <, >, or not-equal. Getting these two statements right determines everything that follows — which tail (or tails) of the sampling distribution you look at, how the p-value is computed, and what your conclusion is allowed to say.

In engineering practice, the claims you test are concrete: a supplier says a batch of fasteners averages at least a given tensile strength, a process engineer says a redesign reduced mean cycle time, a quality team says fill volume is on target and not drifting in either direction. Each of these maps to a different hypothesis form. A claim about being "on target" leads to a non-directional, two-tailed test; a claim about an improvement or a minimum guarantee leads to a directional, one-tailed test. The direction must be decided from the research question before the data are examined — choosing a tail after seeing which way the sample went is one of the most common and most serious errors in hypothesis formulation.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: (1) where the equality sign always lives, (2) how the wording of a claim ("at least," "more than," "differs from," "has changed") signals the direction, and (3) how the choice of one or two tails changes the shaded rejection region and the p-value calculation. Try writing H0 and H1 yourself from each spoken claim before the narrator does, then check your answer.

Test Statistics and Sampling Distributions

Introduction

A test statistic is a single number computed from your sample that measures how far the sample result sits from what the null hypothesis predicts, expressed in units of standard error. Because every test statistic is compared against a reference distribution — the standard normal curve for a z-test, a t-distribution with a particular number of degrees of freedom for a t-test — you cannot interpret the number without knowing which sampling distribution it belongs to. The sampling distribution is what turns an abstract distance into a probability statement, and that probability is the p-value.

In this course the practical skills are: identify what is known and unknown about the population (is sigma given, or only the sample standard deviation s?), pick the matching test statistic, compute it correctly, determine the degrees of freedom when using t, and convert the result into a p-value you can compare against alpha. These steps sit at the heart of nearly every engineering hypothesis test you will run, from verifying a manufacturing tolerance to checking a vendor's performance claim.

As you watch, keep three questions in front of you: What is being subtracted from what in the numerator? What goes in the denominator, and why is it a standard error rather than a standard deviation? And which curve is the resulting value being compared against? If you can answer those for each example, the formulas stop being things to memorize and become things you can reconstruct.

P-Values and Significance Levels

Introduction

A hypothesis test ends with a number — the p-value — and a decision rule built around a threshold you choose before you look at the data. The p-value answers a narrow but useful question: if the null hypothesis were true, how likely is it that we would see a sample result at least as extreme as the one we actually got? The significance level, alpha, is the amount of risk you are willing to accept of rejecting a true null hypothesis. Comparing the two gives you a defensible, repeatable decision rule: reject the null when p is less than or equal to alpha, otherwise fail to reject it.

This matters in engineering because conclusions about material strength, process yield, calibration drift, or supplier quality are rarely obvious from the raw numbers. A p-value lets you state how much evidence your data provides against a claim, and alpha forces you to decide in advance how strong that evidence must be rather than rationalizing after the fact. Equally important is knowing what a p-value is not: it is not the probability the null hypothesis is true, not the probability your result happened by chance, and not a measure of how large or important an effect is.

As you watch, pay attention to three things. First, the exact wording used to define the p-value — the conditional phrasing "assuming the null is true" does a lot of work. Second, how one-tailed and two-tailed situations change the tail area being computed. Third, how each presenter translates the final comparison back into a sentence about the original claim, since on exams and in practice you will be graded on the interpretation as much as the arithmetic.

Applying Common Hypothesis Tests

Introduction

In earlier parts of this module you learned the logic of hypothesis testing: stating a null and alternative hypothesis, choosing a significance level, computing a test statistic, and deciding whether the evidence is strong enough to reject the null. This topic turns that logic into practice. Engineering data arrives in many shapes — one sample compared to a specification, two independent production lines, before-and-after measurements on the same units, counts in categories, or three or more treatment groups — and each shape calls for a different test.

The practical skill is matching the data scenario to the right procedure. Ask yourself: Is the response measured on a continuous scale or is it a count in categories? How many groups are being compared? Are the observations in the two groups independent, or are they naturally paired? Answering those questions leads you to the one-sample t-test, the two-sample t-test, the paired t-test, a chi-square test, or ANOVA. Getting this choice wrong invalidates everything that follows, no matter how carefully you compute the arithmetic.

The videos below focus on two of the trickier cases: paired data and categorical count data. While watching, pay attention to how each hypothesis is worded, where the degrees of freedom come from, how the test statistic is assembled from the sample values, and — most importantly — how the final numerical result is translated back into a plain-language conclusion about the original question. Try pausing before the solution is revealed and working the example yourself.

Type I and Type II Errors

Introduction

Every hypothesis test ends with a decision — reject the null hypothesis or fail to reject it — and that decision can be wrong in two distinct ways. A Type I error (a false positive) happens when we reject a null hypothesis that is actually true; its probability is the significance level, alpha, that we choose before collecting data. A Type II error (a false negative) happens when we fail to reject a null hypothesis that is actually false; its probability is called beta, and the complement, 1 minus beta, is the power of the test.

This matters in engineering because the two errors usually carry very different costs. Falsely concluding that a new alloy is stronger, that a process change reduced defects, or that a contaminant level exceeds a limit leads to one kind of expensive mistake; failing to detect a real problem leads to another. Choosing alpha, sample size, and effect size is therefore an engineering judgment about which error you can least afford, not just a statistical formality.

As you watch, keep track of four things: the 2-by-2 table of truth versus decision, which error alpha controls and which beta controls, why lowering alpha tends to raise beta when sample size is fixed, and how increasing sample size (or effect size) raises power without that trade-off. Also notice the careful wording used for conclusions — we never "accept" the null hypothesis, we only fail to reject it.

Interpreting and Communicating Results

Introduction

Running a hypothesis test is only half the job. The other half — the half that engineering reports, design reviews, and regulatory submissions actually depend on — is stating what the test does and does not tell you. A p-value is a conditional probability computed under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true; it is not the probability that the null is true, not the probability you made a mistake, and not a measure of how large or important an effect is. Writing conclusions that respect those boundaries is a skill you practice deliberately.

This topic pulls together the pieces: phrasing a conclusion in terms of the original engineering claim rather than in bare symbols, reporting the test statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, and significance level so a reader can check your work, separating statistical significance from practical significance (a 0.2 psi difference can be highly significant with a large enough sample and still be irrelevant to the design), and being honest about Type I and Type II error risk and about the assumptions your test required.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to the exact wording used at the moment of the conclusion. Notice whether the speaker says "reject the null" or "prove the alternative," whether assumptions are checked before results are trusted, and how limitations are flagged. Try restating each conclusion in your own words for a non-statistician reader — that translation is what you will be graded on.
