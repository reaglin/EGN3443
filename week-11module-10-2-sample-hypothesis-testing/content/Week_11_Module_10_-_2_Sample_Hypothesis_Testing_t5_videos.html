Introduction

Many engineering questions come down to comparing two success rates: the defect rate from two production lines, the pass rate of two inspection methods, or the proportion of users who complete a task with two different interface designs. The two-proportion z-test gives you a formal way to decide whether an observed difference between two sample proportions is large enough to be considered real, or whether it is within the range you would expect from sampling variability alone.

The mechanics follow the same five-step logic you used for means, with a few specifics. You state hypotheses about p1 - p2 (usually H0: p1 = p2 against a one- or two-sided alternative), check that the samples are independent and random and that each group has enough expected successes and failures for the normal approximation to hold, pool the two samples into a single estimate of the common proportion under H0, form the z statistic by dividing the observed difference by its standard error, and convert z into a p-value for a decision in context.

As you watch, pay close attention to two things that students most often get wrong. First, why the pooled proportion is used in the test statistic (because H0 assumes the two proportions are equal) even though the unpooled standard error is used for a confidence interval. Second, how the tail area is computed: one-tailed versus two-tailed p-values, and how to read them from a z table or software. Try pausing each example before the arithmetic and working it yourself.
