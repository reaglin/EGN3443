Foundations of Two-Sample Hypothesis Testing

Introduction

Up to this point you have tested claims about a single population — one mean, one proportion, one variance. Engineering questions, though, are usually comparative: Does the new alloy last longer than the old one? Does supplier B deliver more consistent tolerances than supplier A? Did the redesigned process reduce defect rates? Two-sample hypothesis testing is the formal machinery for answering these questions, and it rests on one key idea: instead of comparing a sample statistic to a fixed claimed value, you compare two samples to each other and ask whether the observed difference is larger than what random sampling variability alone would plausibly produce.

This topic lays the conceptual groundwork for everything that follows in the module. You will see how the null hypothesis is expressed as a statement of no difference (typically mu1 - mu2 = 0 or p1 - p2 = 0), how the alternative encodes the direction of the engineering question (two-tailed for 'is there any difference?', one-tailed for 'is it larger/smaller?'), and how the same logic of assume-the-null, measure-the-discrepancy, quantify-the-surprise carries over from one-sample testing. You will also meet the critical design distinction between independent groups (two separate, unrelated sets of units) and paired groups (the same unit measured twice, or units deliberately matched), because that distinction determines which test you may legitimately use later in the module.

The videos below are short refreshers and framing pieces rather than full derivations. As you watch, keep three questions in mind: What exactly is the parameter being compared? How would I write the null and alternative for the engineering question in front of me? And what would the decision rule be if the data turned out to be surprising under the null? Note that the videos are framed largely in one-sample or general terms — your job is to translate each idea into the two-sample setting, replacing 'the mean' with 'the difference between two means.'

Independent vs. Paired Samples

Introduction

Up to this point you have tested claims about a single population. Two-sample hypothesis testing extends that work to comparisons: Is one manufacturing process faster than another? Does a coating reduce corrosion? Before you can choose a formula, though, you must answer a prior question about study design: are the two sets of measurements independent, or are they paired?

Independent samples come from two separate groups of units, where knowing a value in one group tells you nothing about any particular value in the other; the groups can even differ in size. Paired (dependent) samples consist of matched observations, such as the same specimen measured before and after treatment, the same subject tested under two conditions, or units deliberately matched on characteristics that affect the response. With paired data you analyze the differences within pairs, which removes unit-to-unit variability and often gives a more sensitive test with n - 1 degrees of freedom based on the number of pairs, not the total number of measurements.

As you watch, focus on the diagnostic questions each presenter uses to classify a design, how the data are organized in each case (two columns of raw values versus one column of differences), and how the choice changes the test statistic, the standard error, and the degrees of freedom. Pay attention to the real-world examples: being able to recognize the design from a verbal description of an experiment is exactly the skill assessed in this module.

Assumptions and Conditions for Two-Sample Tests

Introduction

Every two-sample hypothesis test rests on a set of assumptions. Before you can trust a p-value or a confidence interval for the difference between two means, you need to know that the data were collected by random sampling, that observations within each group are independent of one another, that the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal (either because the population is roughly normal or because the sample is large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to apply), and — for the pooled-variance t-test — that the two population variances are comparable.

These conditions are not bureaucratic checkboxes; they determine which test you are allowed to use. The same data might call for a pooled t-test, a Welch (unequal-variance) t-test, a paired t-test, or a nonparametric alternative depending on how the data were gathered and what the diagnostics show. In this course you will be expected to state the assumptions, check them with plots and formal tests, and then justify your choice of procedure.

The videos below walk through complete t-test workflows in R, beginning with assumption checking before any test statistic is computed. As you watch, pay attention to the order of operations: identify the design first, verify conditions second, run the test third, and interpret last. Note which graphical and numerical diagnostics are used (for example normality checks and variance comparisons) and how the presenter's conclusion about the assumptions changes the test that gets selected.

Comparing Two Means

Introduction

Up to this point you have tested claims about a single population mean. Many engineering questions, however, involve a comparison: does a new alloy have a higher average tensile strength than the current one, does a revised process reduce average cycle time, does a calibration procedure change the average reading of an instrument? Comparing two means is the natural extension of one-sample hypothesis testing, and the mechanics are familiar — state hypotheses about the difference between the means, compute a t statistic, find a p-value, and decide.

The key new decision is which two-sample procedure applies. If the two sets of measurements come from separate, unrelated units, you use an independent samples t-test, which requires attention to whether the two population variances can be treated as equal (pooled) or not (Welch-type). If each measurement in one group is naturally matched to a measurement in the other — the same specimen before and after treatment, the same part measured by two gauges — the data are paired, and you analyze the differences with a one-sample t-test on those differences. Choosing the wrong test misstates the standard error and can lead to the wrong conclusion.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how the speaker identifies the design (independent versus paired) from how the data were collected, what assumptions are checked or stated before the test is run, and how the numerical output — test statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, and confidence interval — is translated back into a statement about the engineering question. Try to reproduce at least one example by hand or in a spreadsheet so the formulas, not just the software output, become familiar.

Comparing Two Proportions

Introduction

Many engineering questions come down to comparing two success rates: the defect rate from two production lines, the pass rate of two inspection methods, or the proportion of users who complete a task with two different interface designs. The two-proportion z-test gives you a formal way to decide whether an observed difference between two sample proportions is large enough to be considered real, or whether it is within the range you would expect from sampling variability alone.

The mechanics follow the same five-step logic you used for means, with a few specifics. You state hypotheses about p1 - p2 (usually H0: p1 = p2 against a one- or two-sided alternative), check that the samples are independent and random and that each group has enough expected successes and failures for the normal approximation to hold, pool the two samples into a single estimate of the common proportion under H0, form the z statistic by dividing the observed difference by its standard error, and convert z into a p-value for a decision in context.

As you watch, pay close attention to two things that students most often get wrong. First, why the pooled proportion is used in the test statistic (because H0 assumes the two proportions are equal) even though the unpooled standard error is used for a confidence interval. Second, how the tail area is computed: one-tailed versus two-tailed p-values, and how to read them from a z table or software. Try pausing each example before the arithmetic and working it yourself.

Interpreting Results and Making Data-Driven Decisions

Introduction

Running a two-sample test is only half the job. Once you have a test statistic, a p-value, and a confidence interval for the difference between two means or two proportions, you still have to answer the question an engineer or a manager actually cares about: does this evidence justify changing a process, a supplier, a design, or a specification? This topic focuses on that final step — translating numerical output into defensible, clearly communicated conclusions.

Three ideas carry most of the weight. First, the p-value is a conditional probability about data given the null hypothesis, not the probability that the null hypothesis is true and not a measure of effect size; misreading it is one of the most common errors in applied statistics. Second, a confidence interval for the difference between groups tells you both whether a difference is statistically detectable and how large it plausibly is, which is exactly what you need to separate statistical significance from practical significance. Third, every conclusion rests on assumptions — independence, sampling method, approximate normality or adequate sample size — and an honest report states those limitations.

As you watch, pay attention to the exact wording each presenter uses when stating a conclusion, and notice how a confidence interval and a hypothesis test at the same confidence level agree with each other. Try rephrasing each interpretation in engineering terms: "the mean fatigue life of alloy B exceeds that of alloy A by somewhere between 200 and 900 cycles," rather than simply "p is less than 0.05."
