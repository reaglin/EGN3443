Introduction

Running a two-sample test is only half the job. Once you have a test statistic, a p-value, and a confidence interval for the difference between two means or two proportions, you still have to answer the question an engineer or a manager actually cares about: does this evidence justify changing a process, a supplier, a design, or a specification? This topic focuses on that final step — translating numerical output into defensible, clearly communicated conclusions.

Three ideas carry most of the weight. First, the p-value is a conditional probability about data given the null hypothesis, not the probability that the null hypothesis is true and not a measure of effect size; misreading it is one of the most common errors in applied statistics. Second, a confidence interval for the difference between groups tells you both whether a difference is statistically detectable and how large it plausibly is, which is exactly what you need to separate statistical significance from practical significance. Third, every conclusion rests on assumptions — independence, sampling method, approximate normality or adequate sample size — and an honest report states those limitations.

As you watch, pay attention to the exact wording each presenter uses when stating a conclusion, and notice how a confidence interval and a hypothesis test at the same confidence level agree with each other. Try rephrasing each interpretation in engineering terms: "the mean fatigue life of alloy B exceeds that of alloy A by somewhere between 200 and 900 cycles," rather than simply "p is less than 0.05."
