Introduction

Up to this point you have tested claims about a single population — one mean, one proportion, one variance. Engineering questions, though, are usually comparative: Does the new alloy last longer than the old one? Does supplier B deliver more consistent tolerances than supplier A? Did the redesigned process reduce defect rates? Two-sample hypothesis testing is the formal machinery for answering these questions, and it rests on one key idea: instead of comparing a sample statistic to a fixed claimed value, you compare two samples to each other and ask whether the observed difference is larger than what random sampling variability alone would plausibly produce.

This topic lays the conceptual groundwork for everything that follows in the module. You will see how the null hypothesis is expressed as a statement of no difference (typically mu1 - mu2 = 0 or p1 - p2 = 0), how the alternative encodes the direction of the engineering question (two-tailed for 'is there any difference?', one-tailed for 'is it larger/smaller?'), and how the same logic of assume-the-null, measure-the-discrepancy, quantify-the-surprise carries over from one-sample testing. You will also meet the critical design distinction between independent groups (two separate, unrelated sets of units) and paired groups (the same unit measured twice, or units deliberately matched), because that distinction determines which test you may legitimately use later in the module.

The videos below are short refreshers and framing pieces rather than full derivations. As you watch, keep three questions in mind: What exactly is the parameter being compared? How would I write the null and alternative for the engineering question in front of me? And what would the decision rule be if the data turned out to be surprising under the null? Note that the videos are framed largely in one-sample or general terms — your job is to translate each idea into the two-sample setting, replacing 'the mean' with 'the difference between two means.'

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

These videos build the conceptual foundation the rest of the module depends on. Precise hypothesis statements and a clear grasp of directional versus non-directional alternatives are prerequisites for selecting and correctly applying the independent-samples t-test, the paired t-test, and the two-proportion z-test (MO1, MO3, MO4), since the structure of the hypotheses signals which design and parameter are in play. The emphasis on assuming the null and asking how surprising the data would be under that assumption is the reasoning you will use when checking assumptions before running a test (MO2) and when interpreting p-values and confidence intervals in engineering context (MO5). The videos do not perform two-sample calculations themselves — treat them as the framing layer, and expect the module's worked examples and readings to supply the independent-versus-paired decision rules and the test statistic formulas.
