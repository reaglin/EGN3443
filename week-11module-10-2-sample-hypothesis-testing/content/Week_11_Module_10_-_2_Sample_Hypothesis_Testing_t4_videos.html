Introduction

Up to this point you have tested claims about a single population mean. Many engineering questions, however, involve a comparison: does a new alloy have a higher average tensile strength than the current one, does a revised process reduce average cycle time, does a calibration procedure change the average reading of an instrument? Comparing two means is the natural extension of one-sample hypothesis testing, and the mechanics are familiar — state hypotheses about the difference between the means, compute a t statistic, find a p-value, and decide.

The key new decision is which two-sample procedure applies. If the two sets of measurements come from separate, unrelated units, you use an independent samples t-test, which requires attention to whether the two population variances can be treated as equal (pooled) or not (Welch-type). If each measurement in one group is naturally matched to a measurement in the other — the same specimen before and after treatment, the same part measured by two gauges — the data are paired, and you analyze the differences with a one-sample t-test on those differences. Choosing the wrong test misstates the standard error and can lead to the wrong conclusion.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how the speaker identifies the design (independent versus paired) from how the data were collected, what assumptions are checked or stated before the test is run, and how the numerical output — test statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, and confidence interval — is translated back into a statement about the engineering question. Try to reproduce at least one example by hand or in a spreadsheet so the formulas, not just the software output, become familiar.
