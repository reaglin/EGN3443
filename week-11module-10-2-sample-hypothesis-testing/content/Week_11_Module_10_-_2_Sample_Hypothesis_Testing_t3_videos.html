Introduction

Every two-sample hypothesis test rests on a set of assumptions. Before you can trust a p-value or a confidence interval for the difference between two means, you need to know that the data were collected by random sampling, that observations within each group are independent of one another, that the sampling distribution of the mean is approximately normal (either because the population is roughly normal or because the sample is large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to apply), and — for the pooled-variance t-test — that the two population variances are comparable.

These conditions are not bureaucratic checkboxes; they determine which test you are allowed to use. The same data might call for a pooled t-test, a Welch (unequal-variance) t-test, a paired t-test, or a nonparametric alternative depending on how the data were gathered and what the diagnostics show. In this course you will be expected to state the assumptions, check them with plots and formal tests, and then justify your choice of procedure.

The videos below walk through complete t-test workflows in R, beginning with assumption checking before any test statistic is computed. As you watch, pay attention to the order of operations: identify the design first, verify conditions second, run the test third, and interpret last. Note which graphical and numerical diagnostics are used (for example normality checks and variance comparisons) and how the presenter's conclusion about the assumptions changes the test that gets selected.
