Foundations of Regression Validation

Introduction

Fitting a regression line is only the first half of the job. Regression validation is the set of practices engineers use to ask whether a fitted model is actually trustworthy: Do the assumptions behind the least-squares machinery hold for this data? Does the model describe the sample well, and will it still perform acceptably on data it has never seen? These two questions — reliability on the data you have and generalizability to data you don't — are distinct, and a model can succeed at one while failing badly at the other.

In this course, validation matters because engineering decisions get made from model predictions: sizing a component, forecasting a load, setting a process tolerance. A model with an impressive R-squared can still produce systematically wrong predictions if the residuals show curvature, if variance grows with the fitted values, or if the model has simply memorized noise in a small sample. Residual analysis is the main diagnostic tool for the first family of problems; resampling methods such as cross-validation address the second. Together they feed a cycle: fit, diagnose, refine, re-check.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, what specific quantity is being used to judge the model, and what does it actually measure? Second, which plot or procedure would reveal each core assumption violation — linearity, constant variance, independence, normality? Third, how does a validation finding translate into a concrete next step, such as transforming a variable or changing the functional form?

Assessing Regression Model Assumptions

Introduction

Fitting a regression line is only half the job. The least-squares estimates, the standard errors, the t-tests, the F-test, and every prediction interval you build all rest on a set of assumptions about how the data were generated: that the mean response is a linear function of the predictors, that the error variance is constant across the range of fitted values (homoscedasticity), that the errors are independent of one another, and that they are approximately normally distributed. If those assumptions hold, the inference you report is trustworthy. If they do not, the fitted line may still minimize squared error while the p-values, confidence intervals, and predictions it supports are misleading.

In this module the main diagnostic tool is the residual — the difference between an observed response and the value the model predicts. Residuals are what we actually have access to, and the way they are patterned carries information about the unobservable errors. A plot of residuals against fitted values reveals curvature (a linearity problem) and fanning or funneling (a variance problem); a plot of residuals against time or observation order reveals dependence; a histogram or normal probability plot of residuals addresses the normality condition. Learning to read these plots is a skill you build by looking at many of them.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: what each specific plot is designed to detect, what the "good" (random, structureless) version of that plot looks like, and what the failure modes look like. Also notice how the same diagnostic logic is carried out in different software environments — the computations differ, but the questions being asked of the data are identical. Finally, keep in mind the consequence side: a violation of homoscedasticity damages your standard errors and intervals, while a violation of linearity damages the fitted relationship itself, and the two call for different remedies.

Residual Analysis

Introduction

A regression line gives you a prediction for every observation, but the quality of a model lives in what the line misses. A residual is simply the observed value minus the predicted value — the vertical distance from a data point to the fitted line. Individually, residuals tell you how far off a single prediction was; collectively, their pattern tells you whether the model you chose was the right one at all.

In this course, residual analysis is the main practical tool for checking the assumptions behind least-squares regression. Fitting a line is easy and software will always produce one, even when a straight line is a poor description of the relationship, even when the spread of errors grows with the predictor, and even when one unusual point is dragging the slope around. Residual plots — most commonly residuals against fitted values or against the predictor — make these problems visible, because a well-behaved model should leave behind residuals that look like structureless, evenly scattered noise centered on zero.

As you watch the videos below, keep a short checklist in mind. Is there curvature or any systematic shape in the residual plot (a sign of non-linearity)? Does the vertical spread stay roughly constant across the range of fitted values (homoscedasticity) or does it fan out? Are there a few points with unusually large residuals, and would removing them noticeably change the fit (outliers versus influential points)? And do the residuals look approximately normally distributed when you histogram them or plot them on a normal probability plot? Also pay attention to the mechanics: how residuals are actually computed and how software produces these plots for you.

Cross-Validation Techniques

Introduction

When you fit a regression model, the fit statistics you compute on the data used for fitting (R-squared, residual standard error, training error) are optimistic. The model has already seen those points, so it can partly memorize their noise. Cross-validation is the family of techniques that answers a different and more useful engineering question: how accurately would this model predict a new observation it has never seen?

This topic covers the main approaches in increasing sophistication. The holdout method splits the data once into a training set and a test set. K-fold cross-validation rotates the role of the test set across k partitions so every observation is predicted exactly once out-of-sample, then averages the error. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) is the extreme case where k equals the sample size. Each choice trades off bias, variance, and computational cost, and each is appropriate in different situations — a distinction you are expected to be able to make.

While watching, focus on three things: the mechanics of how data are split and reused, why the estimated test error behaves differently than the training error, and how the resulting cross-validation curve is read when comparing candidate models of different complexity. That last point connects directly to model refinement — choosing which predictors or functional forms to keep is a decision you can justify with cross-validated error rather than in-sample fit alone.

Identifying and Diagnosing Model Weaknesses

Introduction

Fitting a regression model is only the first step; deciding whether that model can be trusted is the real engineering work. A model can have a high R-squared and still be built on unstable coefficients, be driven by a handful of unusual data points, or hide predictors that duplicate each other's information. This topic gives you a systematic way to look for those weaknesses before you report a result or use a model to make a design or process decision.

Three problems come up again and again. Multicollinearity occurs when predictor variables are strongly related to one another, which inflates standard errors and makes individual coefficient estimates unstable and hard to interpret; the variance inflation factor (VIF) is the standard numerical check. Outliers are observations with unusually large residuals — they sit far from the fitted line. Influential observations are points whose removal would noticeably change the fitted model, and these are usually points with high leverage (an unusual combination of predictor values) combined with a sizable residual. Outlier and influential are not the same thing, and keeping that distinction straight is one of the main goals of this week.

As you watch, build yourself a checklist: what quantity is being computed, what plot displays it, and what cutoff or rule of thumb signals a problem. Pay attention to how leverage, residuals, and influence relate to each other, and think about what you would actually do next — drop a variable, transform it, re-examine the data point, or collect more data. Also notice the consequences of ignoring these issues: misleading p-values, coefficients with the wrong sign, and predictions that fail on new data.

Model Refinement and Decision-Making

Introduction

Validation tells you whether a regression model is trustworthy; refinement is what you do about it. This topic closes the loop: once residual plots, diagnostic statistics, and cross-validation results have revealed a problem — curvature in the residuals, non-constant variance, a redundant or collinear predictor, an influential point, or poor out-of-sample performance — you have to choose a response. Typical responses include dropping or adding variables, transforming the response or a predictor, changing the functional form, or accepting the model as it stands because it is good enough for its intended use.

In this course, refinement matters because engineering models are built to support decisions, not to win a contest for the highest R-squared. A slightly less accurate model that uses measurable, inexpensive inputs and is easy to explain may be the better engineering choice. You will also see that refinement is iterative: each change produces a new model that must be re-validated, and you need a stopping rule so the process converges rather than drifting into overfitting the data you happen to have.

While watching the videos, pay attention to three things: what evidence triggers a particular refinement, how competing models are compared on a common footing (information criteria, adjusted fit measures, or out-of-sample error), and what the trade-off is between fit and complexity. Keep asking: would this change actually make the model more useful to the engineer who has to apply it?
