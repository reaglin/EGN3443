Introduction

The Poisson distribution models the number of times an event occurs within a fixed interval of time, length, area, or volume — the number of defects per meter of weld, calls arriving at a help desk per hour, or particles detected per second. Instead of a fixed number of trials like the binomial distribution, the Poisson distribution is built around a single rate parameter, lambda, which represents the average number of occurrences per interval.

In this course the Poisson distribution matters for two reasons. First, it is the natural model whenever you are counting rare events over a continuum, which describes a large share of reliability, quality control, and queueing problems in engineering. Second, it has an unusually clean structure: the probability mass function depends on a single parameter, and both the mean and the variance equal lambda. That makes it a good distribution for practicing the general skills of this module — verifying that probabilities sum to one, computing expected value and variance, and interpreting those numbers in context.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: how lambda is identified and rescaled when the interval in the problem differs from the interval in which the rate was reported; the exact form of the probability formula and how factorials and the exponential term are evaluated; and the conditions (independence, constant average rate, events occurring one at a time) that justify using Poisson rather than binomial. Keep a running list of worked examples so you can recognize problem types on an exam.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos move from the structure of the Poisson probability mass function to its parameters and applications. The formula-focused segments support MO4 by showing how to identify lambda correctly for a given interval and compute exact probabilities, and they reinforce MO1 by demonstrating that Poisson probabilities are non-negative and sum to one across all non-negative integer counts. The mean-and-variance video addresses MO2 and gives you the key fact that both equal lambda. The discussion of when Poisson applies — independent events at a constant average rate over a continuum, with no fixed number of trials — equips you to meet MO3 by distinguishing Poisson from binomial scenarios, and the worked engineering and real-world examples across all three videos model the contextual interpretation required by MO5.
