Introduction

The binomial distribution describes the number of "successes" in a fixed number of independent trials when each trial has only two possible outcomes and the probability of success stays the same from trial to trial. Think of testing 50 manufactured parts for defects, sampling 20 solder joints for failure, or recording whether each of 100 transmitted packets arrives intact. Because so many engineering inspection, reliability, and quality-control problems have exactly this structure, the binomial is one of the most used discrete models you will meet in this course.

In this topic you will learn to check the four defining conditions (fixed n, two outcomes, constant p, independent trials), identify the parameters n and p from a word problem, and apply the formula P(X = x) = C(n,x) p^x (1-p)^(n-x). You will also compute the mean, np, and the variance, np(1-p), and use the standard deviation to judge how much variability to expect around the average number of successes.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: where the binomial coefficient comes from and why it counts arrangements; how the speaker extracts n and p from the wording of a problem; and how the mean and standard deviation are interpreted in context rather than just computed. Keeping those three threads in mind will make the later comparison with the Poisson distribution much easier.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Brunton and Primer develop the binomial probability formula and the conditions behind it, supporting your ability to recognize when a scenario is binomial (MO3) and to compute exact probabilities with correctly identified n and p (MO4), while reinforcing that the probabilities assigned over all possible counts form a valid discrete distribution summing to one (MO1).
