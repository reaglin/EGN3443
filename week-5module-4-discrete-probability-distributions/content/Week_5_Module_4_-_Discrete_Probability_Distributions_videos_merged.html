Introduction to Discrete Probability Distributions

Introduction

Up to this point you have worked with probabilities of individual events. A discrete probability distribution takes that idea one step further: it is a complete accounting of every possible value a random variable can take, together with the probability of each value. If you roll a die, count defective parts in a batch of ten, or tally the number of service calls arriving in an hour, the outcomes are distinct and countable, and the distribution is the table or formula that assigns a probability to each one.

This topic matters because every named distribution you meet later in the module, including the binomial and Poisson, is a special case of the general structure introduced here. Before you can use a formula, you need to be comfortable with the notation (capital X for the random variable, lowercase x for a particular value, P(X = x) for the probability mass function), with the two validity rules (every probability is between 0 and 1, and the probabilities sum to exactly 1), and with recognizing when a quantity is discrete rather than continuous.

While watching, pay attention to three things: how each presenter moves from a verbal description of an experiment to a formal list or table of values and probabilities; the specific language used to distinguish a probability mass function from a probability density function; and the reason probabilities for a continuous variable at a single point behave differently. Keep a pencil handy and try to write out the distribution table for an example of your own as you watch.

Probability Mass Functions and Distribution Properties

Introduction

A probability mass function, or PMF, is the rule that assigns a probability to each possible value of a discrete random variable. If a random variable counts something — defective parts in a batch, cars arriving at an intersection, packets dropped in a network — then its PMF is the complete description of how likely each count is. Written as p(x) = P(X = x), it is the discrete counterpart to the probability density function used for continuous variables.

Two properties make a function a legitimate PMF: every probability must be non-negative (0 is less than or equal to p(x), which is less than or equal to 1), and the probabilities over all possible values must sum to exactly 1. These two conditions are the test you apply whenever you are handed a table or formula and asked whether it represents a valid distribution, or when you must solve for an unknown constant that makes it valid. They also underpin everything later in the module, since expected value and variance are weighted sums that only make sense when the weights are valid probabilities.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: how a PMF is displayed (table, formula, or bar graph), how the summation property is used as an algebraic tool rather than just a definition, and how individual PMF values are interpreted in words — for example, "there is a 12% chance that exactly two units fail." Also notice the distinction between a PMF and a cumulative distribution function (CDF), which accumulates probability up to a value.

Expected Value and Variance of Discrete Distributions

Introduction

Once you can write down a valid probability mass function, the next question is what that distribution actually tells you. Two summary numbers do most of the work: the expected value, E(X), which is the probability-weighted average of all possible values and describes the center of the distribution, and the variance, Var(X), which measures how far outcomes tend to fall from that center. The standard deviation, the square root of the variance, puts that spread back in the original units of the random variable.

These ideas matter in engineering because a single probability rarely answers a design question. You usually need to know the long-run average number of defects, the average cost of a maintenance policy, or the average waiting time, together with how much variability to plan around. Expected value supports the decision; variance and standard deviation tell you how much risk surrounds it.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: the structure of the formulas (sum of value times probability for the mean; sum of squared deviation times probability for the variance, with the equivalent shortcut E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2), the bookkeeping of setting up a table of x, p(x), and x*p(x), and the interpretation step at the end. Expected value is a long-run average, not necessarily a value the variable can ever take, and that distinction shows up often in exam questions.

The Binomial Distribution

Introduction

The binomial distribution describes the number of "successes" in a fixed number of independent trials when each trial has only two possible outcomes and the probability of success stays the same from trial to trial. Think of testing 50 manufactured parts for defects, sampling 20 solder joints for failure, or recording whether each of 100 transmitted packets arrives intact. Because so many engineering inspection, reliability, and quality-control problems have exactly this structure, the binomial is one of the most used discrete models you will meet in this course.

In this topic you will learn to check the four defining conditions (fixed n, two outcomes, constant p, independent trials), identify the parameters n and p from a word problem, and apply the formula P(X = x) = C(n,x) p^x (1-p)^(n-x). You will also compute the mean, np, and the variance, np(1-p), and use the standard deviation to judge how much variability to expect around the average number of successes.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: where the binomial coefficient comes from and why it counts arrangements; how the speaker extracts n and p from the wording of a problem; and how the mean and standard deviation are interpreted in context rather than just computed. Keeping those three threads in mind will make the later comparison with the Poisson distribution much easier.

The Poisson Distribution

Introduction

The Poisson distribution models the number of times an event occurs within a fixed interval of time, length, area, or volume — the number of defects per meter of weld, calls arriving at a help desk per hour, or particles detected per second. Instead of a fixed number of trials like the binomial distribution, the Poisson distribution is built around a single rate parameter, lambda, which represents the average number of occurrences per interval.

In this course the Poisson distribution matters for two reasons. First, it is the natural model whenever you are counting rare events over a continuum, which describes a large share of reliability, quality control, and queueing problems in engineering. Second, it has an unusually clean structure: the probability mass function depends on a single parameter, and both the mean and the variance equal lambda. That makes it a good distribution for practicing the general skills of this module — verifying that probabilities sum to one, computing expected value and variance, and interpreting those numbers in context.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: how lambda is identified and rescaled when the interval in the problem differs from the interval in which the rate was reported; the exact form of the probability formula and how factorials and the exponential term are evaluated; and the conditions (independence, constant average rate, events occurring one at a time) that justify using Poisson rather than binomial. Keep a running list of worked examples so you can recognize problem types on an exam.

Applying Discrete Distributions to Real-World Problems

Introduction

So far this module you have met the discrete distributions one at a time: the Bernoulli and binomial, the geometric and negative binomial, the hypergeometric, and the Poisson. Real problems do not arrive labeled. This topic is about the step that comes first in practice — reading a scenario carefully, deciding which model its assumptions actually match, and then setting up the parameters (n and p, or lambda and the time or space interval) before touching a formula.

Once the model is chosen, the rest of the work is mechanical but easy to get wrong: translating phrases like "at least three," "no more than two," or "the first success" into the correct sum of probability mass function terms, deciding when a complement is faster, and then computing the expected value and variance so you can say something about the long-run behavior of the process, not just a single event. The final step — interpreting the number in context — is what makes the analysis useful to an engineer or a decision maker.

The three videos below are worked-problem and conceptual supports. As you watch the two binomial examples, pay attention to how the speaker identifies n, p and x from the wording of the problem, and how cumulative and "at least" questions are organized. In the third video, focus on the reasoning that links counts of events in an interval to the waiting time between events — that connection is a common source of confusion when you are deciding whether a scenario is Poisson at all.
