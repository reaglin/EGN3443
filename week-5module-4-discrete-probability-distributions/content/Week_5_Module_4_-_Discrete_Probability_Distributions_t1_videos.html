Introduction

Up to this point you have worked with probabilities of individual events. A discrete probability distribution takes that idea one step further: it is a complete accounting of every possible value a random variable can take, together with the probability of each value. If you roll a die, count defective parts in a batch of ten, or tally the number of service calls arriving in an hour, the outcomes are distinct and countable, and the distribution is the table or formula that assigns a probability to each one.

This topic matters because every named distribution you meet later in the module, including the binomial and Poisson, is a special case of the general structure introduced here. Before you can use a formula, you need to be comfortable with the notation (capital X for the random variable, lowercase x for a particular value, P(X = x) for the probability mass function), with the two validity rules (every probability is between 0 and 1, and the probabilities sum to exactly 1), and with recognizing when a quantity is discrete rather than continuous.

While watching, pay attention to three things: how each presenter moves from a verbal description of an experiment to a formal list or table of values and probabilities; the specific language used to distinguish a probability mass function from a probability density function; and the reason probabilities for a continuous variable at a single point behave differently. Keep a pencil handy and try to write out the distribution table for an example of your own as you watch.
