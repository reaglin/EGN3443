Introduction

Once you can write down a valid probability mass function, the next question is what that distribution actually tells you. Two summary numbers do most of the work: the expected value, E(X), which is the probability-weighted average of all possible values and describes the center of the distribution, and the variance, Var(X), which measures how far outcomes tend to fall from that center. The standard deviation, the square root of the variance, puts that spread back in the original units of the random variable.

These ideas matter in engineering because a single probability rarely answers a design question. You usually need to know the long-run average number of defects, the average cost of a maintenance policy, or the average waiting time, together with how much variability to plan around. Expected value supports the decision; variance and standard deviation tell you how much risk surrounds it.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: the structure of the formulas (sum of value times probability for the mean; sum of squared deviation times probability for the variance, with the equivalent shortcut E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2), the bookkeeping of setting up a table of x, p(x), and x*p(x), and the interpretation step at the end. Expected value is a long-run average, not necessarily a value the variable can ever take, and that distinction shows up often in exam questions.
